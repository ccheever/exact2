# LLP 1072.000: Asynchronous timer advances

**Type:** RFC
**Status:** Draft r2 (r1's blind panel, Astra and Grok, folded in: §8; §7.1's open ruling put to a second blind panel, Astra and Grok, whose findings and recommendations are §9). A decision brief for Charlie: whether to reverse LLP 1072 T6's last sentence, "Timer advances stay synchronous". Nothing is implemented.

**Author:** Claude (Opus 5.5) for Charlie Cheever
**Date:** 2026-10-27
**Related:** [LLP 1072](1072-building-rows-off-the-main-thread.rfc.md) T3, T4, T6, §5, §7.1; [LLP 1027.007](1027.007-evaluating-the-module-off-the-owner.rfc.md) P1 and 6d40e9daf7 (asynchronous notices and reply pumps); [LLP 1027.002](1027.002-optional-worker-execution.rfc.md) (worker placement); [LLP 1016.001](1016.001-then-after-an-answer.rfc.md) D3; [LLP 1012](1012-agent-api-v1.spec.md); `bench/bakeoff/specs/watchlist/SPEC.md`, `bench/bakeoff/results/r2*/summary.md`, `~/bakeoff/rounds/r2*/feel/watchlist-exact/feel.txt` and `…/apps/watchlist-exact/`. ## Summary

On iOS a timer's commit runs on the owner thread while main waits. In the
bake-off's watchlist that wait is most of Exact's one remaining scroll hitch.
**Recommended: B, revised.** Every timer advance on iOS is posted to the owner
as frame ticks and reply pumps already are. A `then` alone may advance
synchronously. Before T6 is reversed, Charlie rules on what a tap on a stale
target does (§7).

## 1. What T6 says and why

T6: "An asynchronous fill only creates. … Timer advances stay synchronous."
A timer's commit can destroy any view, including the one under the finger.
If it ran without main waiting, UIKit would keep hit-testing a view the
runner had already forgotten, and a touch in that interval would be refused
as `UnknownView` (`event.rs:1182-1189`). LLP 1072 §7.1 accepts the cost: "A late timer
frame during a fling is the price."

## 2. The evidence

The spec moves about 20 of 260 prices a second, "including while it is being
scrolled". Exact's list hitch by round: r21 79.7 ms/s, r22 16.6, r23 24.9,
r24 0.0, r25 0.7, r26 10.1; UIKit's is 0.0 throughout. In r26, 9.7 of the
10.1 come from late frames that hold a timer's tick. The list also has 2
offset jumps and 1 of 4 flings dropping a frame at the lift. Its scrub hitch
is 23.6 ms/s against UIKit's 4.3, and all four of the scrub's late frames are
ticks. Per late frame, the probe attributes 6–14 ms to app JavaScript and
3–8 ms to Exact's runtime, both while main waits on `exact.owner`, and
5–19 ms to main's own host work over one or two frames. The tick is the
1 Hz `every(1000, tick)`, armed as a run-loop timer (`fireClock`).

**The app sets the size of the wait.** Each round's app is written afresh.
r24's module moves 20 prices and returns 260 small records. r26's rebuilds
all 260 every tick, each with a 60-price history and an SVG `points` string,
for the same visible change. The exact2 pins differ too, so the rounds do
not isolate the app. Elsewhere Exact matches or beats native: in r26, 14 of
15 Exact lists scroll clean against 10 of 15.

## 3. What is already asynchronous

T3's "Nothing else" no longer describes the code. Asynchronous today:
- frame ticks (§0.3 item 3) and canvas draws (§8.5);
- system notices and data-source reply pumps (LLP 1027.007 P1).

A reply resumes app code and can destroy any view while main is free, so
T6's hazard already exists for replies, untested. Today's synchronous path
has a narrow form of it too. `dispatch_at` fires due work before the event
(`advance.rs:43-51`). That work's commit stands even when the event is then
refused (`host.rs:1237`).

## 4. Options

**A. Keep T6 and cut the cost.** Engine work continues. Authors return only
what changed (a pitfall entry), or place a heavy module on a worker (LLP
1027.002) so its answer arrives as an asynchronous reply. The hitch then
depends on what the author writes and knows.

**B. Asynchronous advances, revised.** `Frames.tick` and `fireClock` post the
advance to the owner, as `sendTick` does. At most one advance is in flight,
and a frame skips its advance while one is. The batch joins the publication
queue in owner order (T4). `ownerInFlight` counts it, so events, reads and
`drainFill` wait for it.

`advanceIfDue` runs from `landFill` and `apply` (`Session.swift:641`, `:1082`)
and today advances everything due. It is narrowed. When the earliest due work
is an armed `then` or a queue's `next`, it advances synchronously to that
time only, which keeps LLP 1016.001 D3's answer and `then` in one frame.
Anything else due is posted. This needs one runner query, the kind of the
earliest due work. Agent mode and `EXACT_FILL_SYNC=1` stay synchronous.

**C. Asynchronous only while a scroller moves.** UIKit delivers no row tap
during a drag or deceleration, so the hazard is smallest when the hitch
shows. But Exact's motion, interactive drags and the keyboard hitch too, so
the condition grows toward "always", and each mode change is a drain point to
test. It is B with more cases.

**D. A per-tick budget.** One timer's commit, one JS call, is the whole cost
here, and splitting a commit changes the runner's transaction. Rejected.

## 5. Consequences of B

- **Runner rules:** unchanged; the owner serializes every input. **Observed
  times:** changed on a live clock. Each commit samples the wall clock when
  the owner runs it (`advance.rs:86-104`), and a repeating timer's next beat
  follows its callback's completion (`:590-600`). So `now` inside the action,
  and the next deadline, include queue delay. The agent's seek clock clears
  the live clock and is unaffected (LLP 1012).
- **Lag:** a timer's commit shows after the owner's time for it plus one
  main-queue turn (here 9–20 ms + a turn), not one frame. An `every(16, step)`
  whose commit outlasts a frame fires once for its missed beats
  (`advance.rs:552-568`), so its rate falls. Today it falls too, and main
  drops frames as well.
- **Events against what is shown:** the window between a commit and its
  apply grows from zero to the owner's time plus a turn. §7 lists the cases.
- **Lean semantics:** unchanged. An observed event is an advance then a
  dispatch (`Observe.dispatchAt`). `semantics/README.md:82-86` already places
  a due commit that replaces the target outside an observed step "(the hosts
  differ there too)". A difftest case should pin the iOS outcome: the due
  commit stands and the event is refused.
- **Web:** unchanged. The browser scrolls on its compositor, so a timer's JS
  never holds a scroll there. **macOS, Linux, Windows:** unchanged until
  measured (T11).

## 6. Tests and gate for B

1. **Held publication**, for an advance and for a reply. Hold the batch
   before apply, then deliver touch-down and click, focus, VoiceOver focus,
   a selection drag and IME composition:
   - against a view the commit removes;
   - against a surviving view whose label, bound arguments or input value
     the commit changes.

   Each outcome must match §7's ruling, and none may crash.
2. **Live clock:** a delayed advance's next beat and observed `now`, pinned
   for a short repeating timer.
3. **Gate,** three runs of the bake-off probe on r26's watchlist app:
   - list hitch under 5 ms/s, with zero offset jumps;
   - scrub hitch from timer ticks at zero;
   - no regression on the other 14 lists;
   - the agent's drives and smokes unchanged.
4. **LLP 1072 amended:** T3 and T6 name every asynchronous kind (fills,
   ticks, canvas draws, notices, replies and advances).

Option A's pitfall entry is worth landing regardless.

### 7.1 The open ruling: a tap the owner has overtaken

This is the text the r2 panel reviewed. §9 lists what it found false here; those corrections take precedence.

**The race.** Under B a timer's commit finishes on the owner, then shows a
main-queue turn later: 10–25 ms per advance in r26's watchlist (§2). A
press that reaches the owner in that window runs after the commit, against
state the screen does not yet show. At 1 Hz that is roughly 1–3% of taps,
and it matters only when the tick removed or changed the tapped row.
Today's synchronous host has no such window: a commit that lands between
touch-down and touch-up is applied first, and the touch-up then drops the
press because the presenter no longer holds the view (`PointerIOS.swift:485`,
`presenter?.views[id] === self`), silently.

**What UIKit and SwiftUI do.** A touch and a timer are both main run-loop
sources, and each handler runs to completion, so a timer never runs inside a
touch's delivery. The touch is hit-tested once, at touch-down, and its later
phases go to that view. A timer that fires between touch-down and touch-up
changes the model and the views together on main. The action at touch-up then
reads that model, which is what the next frame shows. The screen can trail
the model by one frame (the render server), but the action never reads a model
the next frame will not show. SwiftUI state lives on the main actor, so it
behaves the same way. A `Timer` in the default run-loop mode does not fire
while a scroll view tracks a drag. An app that wants a ticker during a scroll
schedules it in `.common` modes.

**(a) Refuse and log; web-style delivery queued.** Reverse T6 now.
- *Removed row:* the press reaches the runner, which refuses it as
  `UnknownView` and logs it. The timer's commit stands, and the row
  disappears as its batch applies, about when the finger lifts. The tap does
  nothing.
- *Surviving row:* the action runs with the owner's current values, such as
  the new price, or a different item where a view binds by position. Nothing
  catches it.
- *Determinism and tests:* the runner is unchanged and agent drives are
  synchronous. §6.1's held-publication tests and one difftest case pin the
  refusal. iOS then differs from the web by stated policy.
- *Cost:* the tests only.

**(b) Web-style delivery first, then reverse T6.** On the web, `on()`
advances due work and then runs the listener the element already holds
(`rt.js:898-901`). Its arguments are evaluated when the event runs
(`a_N.t(()=>[…])`, `emit.rs` near the `on` emission) and read the row's
signals. A removed row's signals are marked dead but keep their last values
(`rt.js:134-137`). In effect a removed row runs with its last shown values,
and a surviving row runs with current ones. The web reaches this only for work
that was due but had not yet fired when the event arrived. Once a timer has
run, the browser hit-tests the new DOM.

For iOS, (b) means the runner keeps each destroyed view's handler and its
bound arguments, evaluated at destroy, until main reports that the destroy
has applied. A press on such a view runs that action with those arguments
against current state.
- *Removed row:* the action runs for the row the person saw, which is now
  gone. An action that assumes the row exists, such as opening its detail,
  meets missing data.
- *Surviving row:* as in (a).
- *Determinism and tests:* the runner's dispatch gains a tombstone table and
  the ABI gains an apply acknowledgement. The Lean semantics would have to
  model a step it now places outside an observed step
  (`semantics/README.md:82-86`). Difftest and a web-parity test follow.
- *Cost:* a runner, ABI and semantics change with its own review, taking days.
  T6 waits for it.

**(c) Settle before input** (proposed with this ruling). Make the window
unreachable for input instead of choosing what happens in it.
1. Before main dispatches discrete input, it lands every advance, reply and
   notice in flight (`drainFill`), then resolves the event against the tree it
   now shows. Discrete input means press, pointer, key, text input and IME,
   focus, and VoiceOver activation. A press whose view the landed batch
   removed is dropped on main, as today.

   Main waits at most the remaining owner time of one advance, about 20 ms,
   and only when input coincides with an advance. Scroll reports and
   collection feedback stay unwaited. UIKit delivers no row press during a
   drag or deceleration, so the scroll that B is for never pays this wait.
2. Optionally, no new advance is posted while a press contact is down, from
   touch-down until touch-up, cancel or the pan that claims the touch. A due
   advance is posted at release. This removes most of step 1's waits for
   taps, and delays a tick by at most the length of the press.

What the person experiences:
- *Removed row:* if the commit lands before touch-up, the row vanishes under
  the finger and the tap does nothing. With step 2, a tick that comes due
  during the press waits, and the tap acts on what was shown. Both outcomes
  match today and UIKit.
- *Surviving row:* the action runs with the values on screen at touch-up.

Determinism and tests: outcomes equal today's synchronous host and
`EXACT_FILL_SYNC=1`, so §6.1 asserts "same as synchronous" rather than a new
policy. The runner, the semantics and the web are unchanged. Step 1 also
closes the untested reply and notice hazard of §3.

Cost: host Swift only. A drain at each discrete-input entry runs before
target resolution, and each entry needs a test. An entry that is missed
reopens the race for that kind of input.

## 9. r2's panel on §7.1

Both seats reviewed §7.1 blind, at high effort: Astra
(`llp/reviews/rfc-2026-10-27-1072.000-r2.astra.md`) and Grok
(`…r2.grok.md`). Muse, the third family, is not logged in on this Mac and did
not sit.

**Recommendations.**
- **Astra: (a).** The owner's committed state is authoritative. A removed
  target is refused and logged, and a surviving one runs against current
  state, which accepts the changed-value case explicitly. Neither (b) nor (c)
  as written delivers what it claims, so neither should block reversing T6.
  Web parity is a separate, later decision.
- **Grok: (c′), §7.1's (c) step 1 only.** Land owner work already posted at
  each committed-action entry, then drop a removed row with the existing
  touch-up check (`PointerIOS.swift:485`). Grok would delete step 2 and not
  build (b). Due work not yet posted stays inside `dispatch_at`, as today.
  (a) is coherent if the host will not enumerate its entries.

**Where they agree.**
- (b) as written is not web-style delivery. The web evaluates arguments when
  the listener runs (`emit.rs:1359`), not at destroy. `endAll` marks only a
  row's `item` and `index` dead (`rt.js:136`), and they keep their values
  because `read` returns `n.v` (`rt.js:28-31`). An argument such as
  `row.price * rate` combines the retired row with the current rate. The web
  case is due work that has not yet fired. A committed timer is hit-tested
  against the new DOM, which is exactly iOS's race. Both advise against
  building tombstones for this race.
- (c) does not make the race unreachable:
  - `drainFill` is one `Owner.sync` barrier and waits for the whole owner
    queue, including fills and other sessions' jobs. It is not one advance or
    "about 20 ms".
  - `landFill`'s `advanceIfDue` can post another advance.
  - `dispatch_at` still fires work due at the event's time after main chose
    the target (`advance.rs:43-51`), so a residual `UnknownView` remains
    under every option.
- (c)'s waits can reach scrolling. `pointerdown` and per-frame `pointermove`
  run before the scroll view claims the touch (`PointerIOS.swift:139`). An
  app's `scroll` and `pan` handlers are synchronous owner calls from
  `scrollViewDidScroll` (`Bridge.swift:400-428`).
- §7.1 needs a definition of target resolution for each input family. UIKit
  has already bound the touch to its view, and a text callback carries an
  edit already made. Re-hit-testing could activate a replacement row.

**Where only one seat spoke.**
- Grok: step 2 matches neither today nor UIKit. The clock timer is already
  scheduled in `.common` (`SessionClockTimer.swift:15-18`), so it fires
  during a press and during tracking, and the action then reads the updated
  model. Holding the tick runs the tap against the pre-tick row, a third
  policy. Verified: `RunLoop.main.add(timer, forMode: .common)`.
- Grok: in (a) the removed row disappears one owner turn after the tick,
  which may be just after touch-down, not "about when the finger lifts".
  (a) also logs an `UnknownView` where today's drop is silent.
- Grok (#4), a finding about B itself rather than the ruling: an app with a
  `scroll` or `pan` handler makes a synchronous owner call every tracking
  frame. That call queues behind a posted advance whether or not anything
  drains. B's gate (§6.3) should measure an app with a `scroll` handler, or
  B should not post an advance ahead of such calls.
- Astra: the UIKit/SwiftUI paragraph overclaims. Serialized handlers do not
  guarantee that an action reads the next frame's state, and Apple permits
  mutating `State` from any thread.
- Astra: the "1–3% of taps" figure assumes uniform arrival. It is an
  estimate, not a measurement.

**Corrections to §7.1** (both seats, checked against the code):
- (b)'s web description, as above.
- (c)'s wait is unbounded by one advance and can fall on scrolling.
- Step 2's outcome is not today's.
- SwiftUI state is not confined to the main actor.

**For Charlie.** The panel splits between (a) and a narrowed (c′). Neither
seat recommends (b) as a precondition for reversing T6, and neither
recommends step 2. Choosing (c′) needs the committed-action entries named,
and their waits measured on a scroll, before it can claim parity with
today. Choosing (a) needs only §6.1's tests and the difftest case, and
accepts a logged refusal and current-state delivery as iOS policy.
