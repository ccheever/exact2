## Summary

Charlie's question: against Unreal rather than Godot, what actually differs? The answer this
document records: **the rendering-and-content gap widens by an order of magnitude, and the
agent-facing gap reverses.** Unreal is the strongest renderer and the weakest agent target of the
engines the 1041 series has looked at. Most of what makes Unreal Unreal — the Editor, the binary
asset graph, the console and platform matrix, the replication stack, the marketplace — is either
already on `NOT-DOING.md` or is the exact apparatus LLP 1041 F19 says is not being chased, at ten
times Godot's scale.

The practical consequence is small: **the lane's oracle stays "as well as Godot"** (LLP 1041.000
§6a's twin). Unreal is useful here as the reference for one thing the corpus has trouble sizing —
what the default look costs — and as a counterexample that sharpens why the add-on is shaped the
way LLP 1041 §5 shapes it.

Nothing below is measured. Unreal was not installed, built, or driven. Claims about Unreal come
from public documentation and prior knowledge and are marked where confidence is low.

## 1. Method, and what this is not

Desk comparison only. LLP 1041.003 §2's microfixture method was not run against Unreal: the fleet
builders have no video group and no sudo (LLP 1041 F18), an Editor install is tens of gigabytes,
and the source build needs an Epic account linked to GitHub. Treat §2 and §3 as structural claims
about engine *shape*, not as the measured comparison §1041.003 §6 asks for.

This is also not an adoption question. Nobody proposed building on Unreal; LLP 1041.003's
shortlist is PlayCanvas, Babylon, Godot and Three.js, and Unity is already only a release-triggered
watch. Unreal sits further from this product than Unity does on every axis §1041.003 §1 names.

## 2. Findings — where Unreal is out of reach, and why that is fine

- **U1. The default look, industrialized.** Nanite, Lumen, Chaos, MetaHuman and a decade of
  shipped-title tuning are the product. LLP 1041 F21 argues a comparable renderer is tens of
  thousands of lines and weeks to months — but "comparable" there means comparable to Godot's
  forward+ renderer, not to virtualized geometry and a dynamic GI solver Epic staffs a team
  against. F23's "the default look needs a human eye" is the binding constraint, and it binds
  harder here than anywhere else in the series.
- **U2. Content, not code, is the moat.** Fab, MetaHuman, Quixel-derived libraries, and the
  animation and material content that ships in the engine. LLP 1041 F17's answer — procedural
  meshes, SDFs, shaders as art, synthesized audio, because agents are bad at Blender and good at
  code — is a genuinely different bet, not a cheaper route to the same output. It should be
  argued on its own terms and not as a substitute.
- **U3. Platforms.** Consoles, Windows, Android and XR are first-class. `NOT-DOING.md` excludes
  Windows and Android outright, so LLP 1041 F25 ("the larger gap is hosts, not rendering") holds
  doubled: against Unreal the host gap is most of the gap, and it is a ruled exclusion rather
  than an unbuilt item.
- **U4. Networking.** Actor replication, dedicated servers, the newer replication system, and
  Epic Online Services for identity, matchmaking and sessions. This tree has none of it and
  nothing in the 1041 series proposes any. Any multiplayer consumer makes the comparison
  unanswerable rather than close.
- **U5. Team shape.** Unreal is built for dozens of humans in distinct roles — level designers,
  technical artists, animators — with source control adapted to binary assets and an Editor as
  the shared surface. The add-on in LLP 1041 §5 is built for one person and one agent. These are
  different products that happen to both render triangles.
- **U6. Scale systems.** World Partition, level streaming, HLOD, virtual texturing: the machinery
  for worlds too large to fit in memory. LLP 1041 F23 already parks culling, LOD and streaming as
  "expensive but measurable"; Unreal's versions are years of work each. Nothing in LLP 1041.000's
  test game needs them, which is the point.

## 3. Findings — where an engine grown from this one wins by construction

These are not "wins if we build them." They follow from decisions the tree has already made, and
Unreal cannot adopt them without becoming a different engine.

- **U7. Text versus a binary asset graph.** `.uasset`/`.umap` are opaque; the Editor is the only
  authoring surface, diffs are unreadable, and merges are a tooling problem rather than a text
  problem. LLP 1041.007 and 1041.009 propose the exact inverse — scenes as diffable text checked
  at build. For an agent this is the single largest difference in the document. Against Godot the
  gap is narrow (Godot's `.tscn` is text); against Unreal it is total.
- **U8. The clock.** Unreal's tick is wall time and Chaos makes no cross-platform determinism
  promise *(believed, unverified against current Epic documentation)*. LLP 1041 F1, F13 and F14 —
  a seekable clock the agent holds, seeded randomness as an explicit input, a world hash that says
  four hosts simulated the same game — are the properties LLP 1041 says cannot be retrofitted.
  Unreal is the largest existing demonstration that they cannot.
- **U9. The iteration loop.** Unreal's is minutes: C++ compiles, Live Coding with known limits,
  Editor restarts for some changes, and shader/PSO compilation stutter as a chronic problem
  shipped games still fight. LLP 1041 F4's ~20 ms restart that carries state, and LLP 1009 D5's
  build-time shader enumeration, are the same trade made in the opposite direction: D5 constrains
  what the material system can express, and buys the absence of that stutter. Worth stating
  plainly, because D5 usually reads as pure cost.
- **U10. Game UI.** UMG over Slate is widely held to be Unreal's weakest subsystem, and text,
  layout and accessibility inside a game are where every engine is worst. LLP 1041 F5 and LLP
  1014's canvas children make the app engine *be* the game UI — real layout, real text, real
  hit-testing, accessibility included. This is the one place where the mature engine grown from
  this one would be straightforwardly better than Unreal rather than differently scoped.
- **U11. Size, startup and the web.** Unreal ships tens of gigabytes of Editor and cooked builds
  in the hundreds of megabytes; first-party web export was dropped years ago and has not returned
  *(believed, unverified)*. LLP 1041 F24's "on the web the bar is lower" goes from lower to
  absent, and LLP 1030's signed, digest-addressed delivery has no counterpart at all.
- **U12. One observation contract.** LLP 1012's eight operations are the same on every host,
  with a headless host among them. Unreal's automation is real (the Automation framework,
  Gauntlet, `-ExecCmds`, Python in the Editor) but it is several surfaces with different
  guarantees, and LLP 1041.003 §5's caution applies in both directions: an agent driver is not
  free on either side, and ours costs maintenance too.
- **U13. Scripting shape.** Blueprints are a binary visual graph — the worst of U7 for an agent,
  since the authoring surface is neither text nor code. Verse exists but is UEFN-bound
  *(believed, unverified for current UE releases)*. An engine grown from this one is Rust the
  whole way down, with the module seam LLP 1029.000 already defines.

## 4. What this changes for the lane

Little, which is the useful result.

- **D1 — The oracle stays Godot.** LLP 1041.000 §6a's twin is still the right comparison: an
  engine of comparable scope, text scenes, a real CLI, and a web export to beat. An Unreal twin
  would measure the wrong thing and cost a fleet provisioning lane to build.
- **D2 — Unreal is the reference for U1 only.** When the default look needs a target — materials,
  lighting response, what "looks finished" means — Unreal's published material and lighting
  documentation is a better reference than Godot's. LLP 1041 F22 already names Khronos's glTF
  sample assets and a reference path tracer as the *verification* answer; this is the separate
  question of what to aim at.
- **D3 — U9 and U10 are arguments to reuse, not new work.** Both are already true of the tree.
  They belong in whatever document eventually argues the lane's value, because they are the two
  places where "grown from an app engine" is an advantage rather than a handicap.
- **D4 — Nothing moves on `NOT-DOING.md`.** No finding here proposes work. The take recommended
  in LLP 1041 §8 is unaffected.

## 5. Open questions

1. **Does U2 survive contact with a real game?** LLP 1041.000's test game is small enough that
   procedural content is plausible. The question is whether an agent-authored content pipeline
   holds at the size where Unreal users reach for Fab — and nothing in the series tests that.
2. **Is U10 worth measuring?** A UI-heavy comparison against UMG is cheap relative to a renderer
   comparison and is the claim most likely to be true. It needs a consumer before it is worth a
   fleet lane.
3. **Which of U8, U11 and U13 are still accurate?** Three claims are marked believed-unverified.
   If this document is ever cited in a decision, they need a pass against current Epic
   documentation first.

## Confidence

**High** on everything read from this tree: the LLP 1041 findings cited, `NOT-DOING.md`'s
exclusions, LLP 1009 D5, LLP 1012, LLP 1014, LLP 1030.

**Medium** on Unreal's shape — the Editor-centric workflow, the binary asset graph, Blueprints,
Nanite/Lumen/Chaos, World Partition, the replication stack, the compile and PSO-stutter
iteration costs. Standard, widely documented, but read from prior knowledge rather than checked
on 2026-09-19.

**Low** on U8's determinism claim, U11's web-export claim, U13's Verse availability, and every
size and duration figure. No Unreal artifact was installed, built, run or measured for this
document, and none of the comparative claims in §2 or §3 are numbers.
