## Summary

Weird Waters is a Weird Castle Git deck: a fish under an FFT ocean. The ocean
is a port of a commercial three.js library (Three.js Water Pro): per-frame
compute (spectrum, inverse FFTs, normals, folding, foam, mip chains), a
displaced clipmap with its own vertex and fragment shaders, refraction of the
scene behind the surface, and an underwater post pass. **The engine's renderer
has no place for any of it.** It draws a fixed frame from engine components,
with fixed materials, and keeps its depth and colour to itself. The deck shipped
anyway, with web pixels and native macOS state verified, by using the engine
for everything except drawing: its surface wraps `WorldSurface` for the clock, input, saves,
publications and agent, steps it into a 1×1 target, and renders the whole scene
itself with `exact-gpu`. That works, and it is also a second renderer inside
one game. This document records what was missing, what the workaround costs,
and what held up; 1046.006.000 proposes the hooks.

## 1. What the frame is today

`RendererWithAssets::draw_assets` (`game/render/src/renderer.rs`) encodes one
command buffer in a fixed order: skin-palette compute → one depth pass per sun
cascade → the forward pass (4× MSAA HDR: opaque batches, opaque quads, the sky,
then ordered translucent models and quads) → bloom → ACES into the target.
Every pipeline in it is the engine's: sixteen primitive and effect variants at
startup, and the lazily prepared model family (README "Effects"). A game
influences the picture only through components and resources: `Mesh`,
`Material` (twelve floats, or a glTF material), `Environment`, `Fog`,
`Bloom`, lights, `Sprite` and `Emitter`.

## 2. What the ocean needed that the frame does not offer

- **R1. Compute before drawing.** Per frame: three spectrum evolutions, six
  256-point row and column inverse FFTs in workgroup memory, displacement and
  normal/foam textures, and nine-level mip chains for three cascades and a sky
  map. There is no stage where a game's compute can run, and no way for it to
  own the textures and buffers such a stage would write.
- **R2. A mesh with its own shaders.** The water surface is a
  camera-following clipmap whose vertex shader samples three displacement
  textures hierarchically, and whose fragment shader does Fresnel,
  roughness-aware environment reflection, refraction, subsurface scattering,
  foam, sparkle, Snell's window and total internal reflection. `Material` has
  no custom variant, and model materials are PBR only. LLP 1046 F23 predicted
  this: "build-time shader enumeration … a constraint on the material system".
- **R3. The scene behind the surface.** Refraction needs the opaque scene's
  colour and depth after it is drawn and before the water is. The forward
  pass's colour and depth are MSAA attachments stored with `Discard`. The
  resolved HDR feeds only bloom and tonemapping, and no depth survives the pass.
- **R4. A pass between the scene and the tone curve.** Underwater fog (Beer–Lambert
  over the distance to what each pixel sees), volumetric sun shafts, and a
  per-pixel waterline all belong after the scene and before ACES. The engine
  goes straight from forward to bloom to tone.
- **R5. The engine's camera and light, as data.** A game's shaders need the
  view-projection, camera position, sun direction and colour the engine used
  for this frame, interpolated at this frame's alpha. `FrameInput` exists, but
  inside the renderer. The deck computes its own camera from saved components
  (`prev`/current fields it keeps for interpolation, because the `Feed`'s
  transform histories are GPU-side and private).
- **R6. Presentation state derived on the GPU.** Hulls float on the drawn
  displacement: a compute pass samples the waves under bow, stern and beams,
  springs each hull's height, pitch and roll, and the boat vertex shader reads
  the pose from a storage buffer. No readback, no effect on the simulation. The
  engine's `Presentation` trait (for audio) has the right shape for this
  (`sync(world, …)` after ticks, no saved state), but nothing on the render side
  resembles it.

## 3. The workaround, and what it costs

`WatersSurface` (deck `src/render.rs`) implements `Surface` by delegating every
method to a `WorldSurface<WeirdWaters>`, except `render`. That calls the inner
`render` with `Frame { scale: 1 / max(width, height), .. }` and a 1×1 target,
which advances the simulation (points, not pixels, keep touch regions right),
then reads `sim().world()` and `sim().alpha()` and draws.

- **W1. A second renderer.** The deck re-implements camera, lighting, sky,
  fog, tonemapping, instanced meshes, particles and a render-target cache:
  about 1,300 lines of Rust (`src/render.rs`) and 650 of WGSL (scene, sky,
  particles, post) beside the ocean itself. It gives
  up what the engine already does: cascaded shadows, glTF models and skinning,
  the particle and sprite paths, bloom, placed children, residency and
  readiness reporting, and the perf rings.
- **W2. The engine still renders.** The inner renderer is built and runs its
  forward and tone passes into 1×1 every frame. The output is thrown away.
  Tiny raster area does not remove pipeline creation, uploads, command encoding
  or submission; neither the initialization nor the per-frame cost was measured.
- **W3. Two views of the same world.** Nothing the deck draws is an engine
  entity with `Mesh`, so the agent's world inspection says "8 entities"
  while the screen shows hundreds of fish, kelp and boats. The engine's
  pick and layout cannot see them.
- **W4. Shaders cross a repository boundary by copying.** The shader registry
  is the app module's: `Registry.shaders` is one generated slice, and the bake
  ships only `<app>/gpu/shaders/*.wgsl`, as real files (it refuses symlinks,
  and Contract refuses a `use` that leaves the app directory). A deck's shaders
  and HUD must therefore be copied into the host app before a build (the deck's
  `sync.sh`), and the app's reflection and the deck's reflection generate the
  same interfaces twice. Shader-root packaging would remove the shader copy;
  it would not remove the separate Contract/HUD copy.
- **W5. Tint is not naga.** A fragment function that does an early `return`
  and then calls `textureSample` passed naga at build and failed in Chrome at
  pipeline creation (uniformity), leaving a black canvas. The build-time
  validation of LLP 1009 D5 is necessary but not sufficient for the web.

## 4. What held up

- **K1. The `Surface` seam is enough.** Raw device and queue, the target and
  its format, canvas input, publications, messages and agent requests carried
  a full game with no core change, as LLP 1046.004 L1 predicted.
- **K2. `WorldSurface` composes.** Wrapping it kept the fixed clock,
  `#[live]`/`#[restart]` arguments, saves and dev carries, the HUD bus and the
  agent's eight operations intact. The same inputs gave the same world hash on
  web and native macOS (tick 720, `0x692a6940ddc4d891`).
- **K3. A deterministic CPU approximation serves gameplay.** Gameplay needs the
  surface height at a few points per tick (breaching, the underwater test).
  The deck sums the 860 strongest modes of the same spectrum on the CPU with
  `exact_game::math`, seeded by the same hash with the same dispersion snapping,
  and keeps GPU-only effects (buoyancy) out of the simulation. Presentation
  may read the world; nothing presentational feeds back into it. Matching seeds
  does not make a truncated CPU spectrum identical to the full GPU field;
  height error and CPU cost still need measurement before claiming agreement
  or cheapness.
- **K4. Reflection pays for itself.** Nine shaders, thirty-odd bindings, and
  the vertex layouts came from `exact-gpu-reflect` with no hand-written
  offsets. Every layout mismatch was a build error.
- **K5. WebGPU compute runs under the default limits.** `requested_limits`
  (downlevel defaults plus the adapter's storage counts) admitted 128-wide
  workgroups, 4 KiB of workgroup memory, `rgba32float` and `rgba16float`
  storage textures, and storage buffers in the vertex stage.

## 5. A constraint from licensing

The ocean is a derivative of commercial code whose licence allows it in one
end product, and forbids offering it as a component that others add to their
own works (§3.4 of its agreement). Weird Castle is a platform where people make
decks, so **that code cannot become an engine feature**. It must not be a water
material, a default effect, or anything in `game/`. The general lesson: the
engine should supply *mechanism* (stages, resources, the camera as data) and
leave *content*, licensed or not, in the game. That constraint and the
capability gap point the same way.

## 6. Open

- The size and the frame cost of the workaround were not measured. The GPU
  module is 2,116 KiB (549 KiB gzip) on the web, engine included, and the frame
  was not timed on either host.
- Native macOS pixels were not inspected. The build ran under the agent, and
  its state, tree and hashes were checked, but this machine's session had no
  screen-recording grant, and the offscreen window never got a drawable.
- Resolved 2026-09-23: hooks draw geometry into 4× MSAA attachments and sample
  resolved inputs (1046.006.000 §7). The resulting memory/frame cost remains open.

## 7. Source-review corrections (2026-09-23)

The wrapper proves the need for a render extension, not the proposed hook API.
`World::get_mut`, mutable queries and `resource_mut` accept `&World`, so that
reference alone cannot enforce presentation's read-only boundary. C5's separate
model `DrawInstance` records already exist (`game/render/src/lib.rs`); custom
materials would extend them, not wait for a future consolidation pass.

The current MSAA colour and depth are also **transient** attachments
(`game/render/src/buffers.rs`). Keeping them across passes changes allocation
and bandwidth costs. Weird Waters currently draws its particles **before** the
water snapshot (`src/render.rs`); moving all translucency after water is a visual
change, not automatic preservation of the existing composition. These findings
are addressed in 1046.006.000's proposed contracts and acceptance limits.


## 8. Follow-up

Charlie accepted all four rulings in 1046.006.000 §7. Its §8 records the subsequent
implementation, private consumer migration, measured attachment storage, new
cross-host hashes and remaining platform verification. The missing-API findings
above are retained as historical evidence rather than rewritten as current defects.
