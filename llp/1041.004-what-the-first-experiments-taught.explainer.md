## Summary

In one day, a fresh engine went from nothing to: a deterministic world that hashes
the same on arm64 macOS, x86-64 Linux and Chrome wasm; a renderer that draws
200,000 entities; rapier physics behind the engine's own components with its state
in the save; a synthesized audio path; two small games driven end to end through
the app's eight agent operations on the web and on macOS; saves through the agent;
a dev loop that swaps new logic under a live world in 410 ms; a feel probe; and a
comparison against Godot and three.js on the same small game, judged blind. The
score was **19 of 30 against three.js's 29 and Godot's 26**, and the engine has
been rebuilt around what that score exposed. This document is what was learned, in
the order it would matter to someone doing it again.

## 1. Architectural lessons

**L1. A world is a guest in a canvas — the one idea held.** Every seam the add-on
needed turned out to be a defaulted method on `exact_gpu::Surface` with a non-game
reading: `wants_input`/`input`, `messages`, `agent`, `bind_at`, `published`,
`carry`/`restore`, `Frame::seekable`. None required the core to know what a game
is. The HUD being ordinary Contract UI reading a typed publication
(`exactSurface("world")`) is the thing no other engine gives, and it was also the
thing that scored worst until it was made first-class. *Lesson: the selling point
has to be the shortest path, not merely a possible one.*

**L2. One `Data` trait paid for itself many times.** Save, hash, the agent's JSON,
level data and dev-carry all fell out of one derive with no `syn`. It is why "edit
the logic and keep playing at the same tick" cost 30 lines once the seam existed,
and why cross-architecture bit-exactness could be asserted rather than hoped. Its
one real defect (bytes encoded as tagged numbers, then wrapped again: a 10 MB
physics snapshot costs 40 MB of save) is a format bug, not a design bug.

**L3. Fixed ticks under a host-owned clock, with the GPU interpolating, is the
right split — and it must never self-advance.** The first `clock settle` stepped
the world privately; both reviewers found that the host's clock then fell behind.
The world answers `settleAt`; the host's loop comes back. Feel follows from the same
split: the twins judder on a 120 Hz display (Godot as shipped 1.00, three.js 0.43,
fraction of repeated motion frames 49% and 9.5%) because they draw the last tick;
this engine draws between the last two.

**L4. Quiescence is the subtlest contract in the engine.** Declared by the game
(`busy`) it is silently wrong when forgotten; observed by the engine it is right
only if the observation has three states (`Unknown | Still | Changing`), is never
saved, sees mutation through interior mutability (a mutation epoch bumped by every
mutable lease), sees globals not only locals, sees resources and the RNG, and
treats pause as "time stopped" rather than a false sample. It took three review
rounds. *Lesson: a cache of a derived fact needs an unknown state, and "who can
mutate without telling me" is the first question to ask of any invariant.*

**L5. Interior mutability was the right call for ergonomics and the wrong default
for invariants.** `World` mutates through `&self` with dynamic borrow leases
(hecs-style), so a game can log or publish inside a mutable query. The price is
that the engine cannot see mutation unless every lease reports it — which is what
the mutation epoch and, next, per-page write generations do. The alternative
(`&mut World` everywhere) would have cost the ergonomics the scoring said matter
more. Keep the leases; make them report.

**L6. Delete, measure, replace.** The home-grown soft-step solver was correct and
deterministic and, after six rounds, 4.6× slower than rapier with piles that never
slept. Deleting it and putting rapier behind the same components took a day
because the acceptance tests survived. The adapter around rapier is now the slow
part (a scene rebuilt per query; snapshot bytes serialized whole). *Lesson: keep the
tests as the contract and let executors come and go.*

**L7. Entity index as GPU slot is elegant for cubes and wrong for assets.** It made
uploads a memcpy and batching trivial, and it will not carry glTF (several draws per
object), skinning (local poses must be interpolated before hierarchy composition,
not world transforms after) or particles (a thousand sparks must not be a thousand
entities). A draw-instance record separate from the entity slot was owed before the
asset pipeline and has since been built (`game/render/src/lib.rs`: the record sits
above the entity slot space; multiple model parts do not consume entities); the
batches already indirected through a slot list, so it was a moderate change.

**L8. Dimensions belong to instances, not to mesh identity.** Giving `Mesh` real
sizes fixed a documentation lie that had cost a builder a rebuild, then created a
cache keyed by float bits that a pulsing radius could exhaust in 68 seconds. The
fix — one unit mesh per primitive kind, dimensions in the per-slot record, the
capsule keeping its caps through a cap-sign vertex attribute — made 5,000
differently sized spheres one draw and an animated radius free. *Lesson: what an
author can animate must not be an allocation key.*

## 2. What using it taught (the builders' diaries)

- A fresh builder made Beacons in 8.5 minutes and 258 lines against Godot's 318 and
  three.js's 198 — but needed a 199-line copy of the web build script, wrote
  `busy` three times, hit a wrong `Mesh` doc, waited 63 s per logic edit, and could
  not save through the agent. **None of that was visible from inside the engine.**
  The diaries are the most valuable instrument in the lane.
- The 63 s loop was a core bug, not a game bug: a bake watched its own receipt and
  the web bake passed an explicit dep-info path that defeated cargo's cache. Every
  app in the repository was paying it. The game lane found it because a game's loop
  is measured in seconds.
- After the ergonomics pass, Beacons is 151 lines of logic and its tests read like
  the proof script (`s.key_down("KeyW"); s.run(1500.0)`). It is still more ceremony
  than three.js's 56-line `simulation.js`: spawn tuples where the twin writes a
  literal, queries where it writes fields, a `Follow` component where it eases four
  numbers. Some of that is Rust; some is us; the next score says how much.

## 3. Process lessons

- **Builder, two blind reviewers, an occasional whole-review.** Every change went
  astra → sol + grok (blind, same revision) → fixes. The pattern held on every
  change: the builder finds the design's gaps, the reviewers find the builder's.
  The whole-engine review by a third model found what change reviews structurally
  cannot (format blow-ups, hidden rebuilds, column-wide invalidation, a README that
  had become a history).
- **Run the full checks after every core-touching fix.** A targeted test run missed
  that the typechecker fix had dropped `exactDelivery` from the plan's source table;
  only `cargo test --workspace` caught it.
- **Verify by running, and look at the picture.** Sandboxed builders cannot see the
  GPU; one GPU test had never executed. The shadow acne was in every screenshot and
  in no test until an image-statistics test was written for it.
- **Measure feel, do not argue it.** The judges marked Feel "unverified" for all
  three engines. A probe that plays the same scripted session and reports frame
  intervals, judder and event-to-drawn-state latency turned it into numbers — and
  found that Godot's default is the worst of the three on a 120 Hz display.
- **Fix loops get three rounds.** Physics got six and was deleted. Quiescence got
  three and the third is under review with a stop condition.

## 5. What I would do differently from the start

1. Write the feel probe and the scoring rubric on day one and score the grey box
   before building anything else; the score, not the feature list, ordered the work.
2. Make bytes a `Data` primitive before the first save.
3. Design the draw-instance record before the renderer's first batch.
4. Give quiescence its three states on the first attempt.
5. Ask the whole-engine question ("what would you delete?") after every second
   feature, not after the tenth.
