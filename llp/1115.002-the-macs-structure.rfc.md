## 0. Summary

An Exact app on the Mac behaves like a Mac app since LLP 1115.000's wave 1: Back, the
menus, focus, Return, the alert. It still **looks like an iPhone app in a Mac window**. Shelf
shows a tab bar of three buttons along the bottom edge, the page's title drawn in the
content under a title bar that says "Shelf", iOS's 52-point rows in 26-point rounded cards,
a "New Book" form laid over the library with the library showing through its header, and
list rows that neither highlight nor separate.

This document decides what the same Contract becomes on the Mac, with nothing new for an
author to write:

| The author wrote | On the Mac it is | Decision |
|---|---|---|
| a route `header` (a heading, buttons, a search field) | the window's title and toolbar | D2 |
| a root `tablist` | a sidebar | D3 |
| a list whose rows push a detail | a split view | D4 |
| a vertical `list` | a table, with the Mac's selection | D5 |
| `list appearance="auto"` | the Mac's grouped form: 36-point rows, 10-point cards | D6 |
| a `dialog`, a modal route, a popover | a sheet, a sheet, an `NSPopover` | D7 |

Everything in it was **measured on macOS 27.0.1** with small AppKit and SwiftUI probe apps
(§1), not recalled, except where a line says so. Two of the six need the kernel to learn
something (D6 a table of metrics; D4 and D7.4 a containing block for a subtree, D8); the
others are the host's alone.

## 1. What is there today, and what a Mac app shows

Read at `d2f8a309f`. The findings, with file and function names, every number below and 83
pictures, are kept with the audit's records (`~/audits/exact2-no-tells-2026-10-11/mac-structure/`:
`findings.md`, `probes/`, `out/`, `png/`). Audit IDs: MAC-02 to -05, -07, -26, -30.

**How it was measured.** Each probe is an accessory app, never activated, with its windows
far off screen; pictures are the window server's of the probe's own windows. A window
subclass draws its title bar as key without being key. So anything that needs a real key
window, a real pointer, a material with content behind it, or motion was **not** measured:
focus rings, hover, keys in a list, sheet and sidebar animation, a drag, glass tints, and
how a row behaves when the person returns to it. The display is 1x, so 2x hairlines were
not seen. Only macOS 27 was measured (§5 Q9).

**Title bar and toolbar.** Exact today: `.unifiedCompact`, 40 points, holding only Back,
titled with the app's name. SwiftUI's window: `.unified`, `displayMode` `.iconOnly`, 52
points; the title 15-point semibold after Back; buttons as 36-point glass circles; a search
item 36 high and 240 to 325 wide; no large title anywhere. With `.default` display the bar
shows labels and is 66 points.

**A header's parts in a toolbar** (stage 1, 2026-10-12: probes `header.swift` and
`header2.swift`, which extend the research's `toolbar.swift`; same method, same limits; with
the pictures in `~/audits/exact2-no-tells-2026-10-11/mac-header/`). Each was put beside
SwiftUI's own (`navigationTitle`, `ToolbarItem`, `Menu`, a principal `Picker`,
`.searchable`) in a 900-point window:

- *SwiftUI's toolbar is an `NSToolbar`* whose items are, in order: a `.navigation` item
  (navigational, ahead of the title); the principal item, named in
  `centeredItemIdentifiers`, then a flexible space; the other items; a flexible space and
  the search item. The title is AppKit's, 15-point semibold, and stretches, so items with
  no flexible space before them sit at the bar's end.
- *Search.* `.searchable` is an `NSSearchToolbarItem` holding SwiftUI's own subclass of
  `NSSearchField`, with its own cell and delegate. A subclass the app makes, with its own
  cell class, delegate, target and formatter, is drawn exactly as AppKit's default (x 567,
  325 by 36, a 13-point font and control size 4 in a 900-point window), and
  AppKit leaves the delegate, target and formatter alone. Narrowing, the field is 325 at
  700, about 205 at 560 and a button at 480; `beginSearchInteraction` gives it the first
  responder in a window that is not key.
- *A menu.* SwiftUI's `Menu` in a toolbar shows AppKit's indicator, as an
  `NSMenuToolbarItem` does with `showsIndicator`, its default; without it the item joins
  the next plain item's glass.
- *Segments.* An `NSToolbarItemGroup` of titles, centred, is the same control at the same
  place as SwiftUI's principal `Picker` (x 304, 296 by 36). Narrowing, the group becomes a
  pop-up of the same choices (at 560) before anything overflows, and goes to the overflow
  menu at 420; SwiftUI's picker, a custom view, goes to the overflow menu whole at 560.
- *Glass.* Back and a navigational button after it share one glass capsule, and so do a
  menu item without its indicator and the plain item after it; a menu item with its
  indicator, a prominent item and the search item each have their own. That is AppKit's
  grouping and nothing sets it.
- *Narrowing with everything* (a leading button, segments, a menu, two buttons, search),
  in the order Exact gives the bar: at 1100 the search field is a field, 226 wide; at 900
  it is a button; at 700 the group is its pop-up; at 560 the menu and the buttons are in
  the overflow menu; at 480 the group is too; at 420 the leading button is. Back and the
  search button stay. A title and two buttons alone keep both down to 480.

**Sidebar.** A sidebar item's minimum width is 144; it runs the window's full height,
under the title bar. Rows are 32 points in SwiftUI's sidebar at the medium size; 13-point
text; a symbol of about 15 by 19 points **in the accent**; the selection inset 10 with a
6-point corner, `#0070f5` with white text when emphasized, grey when the window is not key.

**A source list's rows, and a sidebar on a narrow window** (stage 2, 2026-10-12: probes
`sidebar.swift`, `sidebar2.swift` and `sidebar3.swift`, which extend the research's
`split.swift`; same method, same limits).

- *Rows.* An `NSTableView` in the `.sourceList` style with the system's row size style
  (`rowSizeStyle = .default`) follows the person's Sidebar icon size: **24, 32 and 40
  points** for small, medium and large, with 11-, 13- and 15-point text and symbols
  configured at the same sizes; unset, as on this Mac, it is medium. The 24 points r1
  called AppKit's default was a table left at a custom row height. Given a cell's
  `imageView` and `textField` with no frame and no constraint, AppKit places both: the
  symbol centred in a column at the leading edge, every title at one x (44 at medium), the
  selected title semibold while emphasized, the symbols in the accent with no tint set.
- *The first row's place.* AppKit decides a source list's top padding when the list enters
  its window. In a window that is full-size-content then, the first row starts where the
  bar ends (40 under `.unifiedCompact`; SwiftUI's starts at its 52). In one whose content
  stops under the title bar the list is given **10 points** above its first row, and keeps
  them when the bar comes over it afterwards, through `reloadData` and `tile` (probes
  `sidebar5.swift`, `sidebar6.swift`).
- *Width.* A sidebar whose view names no size opens **144 wide at every window width**
  from 420 to 2000 (`preferredThicknessFraction` 0.15 did not widen it), as SwiftUI's
  does. The research's 220 was its probe's own view.
- *Narrowing.* The pane with the lower holding priority gives up width first: the content
  (250), then the sidebar (260). With a minimum on the content pane (320 here, as a
  `minimumThickness` or a constraint alike) the content narrows to it, then the sidebar
  narrows from its width to 144, and then **the window stops: 464, the two minimums
  together, is the window's minimum width**, for a programmatic size too. A sidebar
  squeezed to 144 stays 144 when the window widens. With no minimum on the content it goes
  to nothing while the sidebar keeps its width.
- *No collapse and no overlay* in a window that is not full screen.
  `minimumThicknessForInlineSidebars` is automatic by default, and set to 600 it changed
  nothing at any width down to 150. AppKit documents the automatic collapse and the
  overlay for full screen, which an off-screen probe cannot enter: **not measured**.
- *The toolbar's button* (`toggleSidebar:`, answered by the split view controller) hides
  and shows the sidebar in place at every width. Hidden, the window's minimum is the
  content's alone (320); shown again there, **the window widens** to hold both (320 to
  465). A sidebar the person hid stays hidden through a narrowing and a widening.
- *The toolbar's style.* `.unifiedCompact` holds a sidebar as `.unified` does: full
  height under a 40-point bar in place of 52, the sidebar's button after the traffic
  lights, the items after the tracking separator starting at the content's edge.
- *What is remembered.* `autosaveName` keeps each pane's frame and whether it is
  collapsed (`NSSplitView Subview Frames <name>` in the app's defaults): the width and
  the hiding both come back at the next launch.
- *SwiftUI at the same widths* (`NavigationSplitView` and `TabView` with
  `.sidebarAdaptable`, hosted off screen, not in a scene): the sidebar 144 at every
  width from 150 to 1400, never collapsed, and the window's minimum 144. Its content pane
  is the whole window with the sidebar over it, where AppKit's is beside the sidebar.
- *A row's badge.* SwiftUI's `.badge` on a sidebar row is a small number in the secondary
  colour at the row's trailing edge. `NSButton` with the `.badge` bezel draws the same
  thing in AppKit: secondary at rest, white in an emphasized selected row.

**Lists.** `NSTableView.style = .automatic` resolves to `.inset`: selection inset 10 with a
6-point corner. A SwiftUI `List` sizes a row to its content and draws a 1-point separator
from 16 to 16, none beside a selected row; a `List` of navigation links draws **no
disclosure chevron**. **On mouse-down a row is selected and drawn emphasized**, in AppKit
and in SwiftUI alike: that is the Mac's press for a row.

**Grouped form.** SwiftUI's grouped `Form` has no AppKit table under it: it is painted.
Rows are 36 points (a picker's 38, a stepper's 40, a button's 44; Exact: 52), cards have a
10-point corner (26), inset 20 (16) and **at most 704 wide, centred**, 10 apart, or 56 with
a header; the card is `#f9f9f9` on the white window (dark `#272727`), which is why Exact's
cards vanish when their surfaces are written as roles: on macOS 27 the window and control
backgrounds are both white. Headers are 13-point bold in the label colour; a toggle is the
small switch; a field is borderless and right-aligned; **a destructive action is a bordered
push button, left-aligned and not red**.

**Sheets and popovers.** A sheet is a separate window centred on its parent on both axes,
with a corner of about 16, the parent dimmed by AppKit. A SwiftUI sheet **sizes to its
content** (100 by 80 for one line of text; 470 by 226 for a two-field form), with its title
at the top left and **Cancel then Save at the bottom right**. An `NSPopover`'s window is its
content plus 13 points a side.

**The same two in a window really made key** (stage 5, 2026-10-12; the probe, its output and
its pictures are in `…/mac-sheets/`). The first probe's windows were never key, so nothing
about the keyboard had been measured. Keys and clicks here were `NSEvent`s through the
application; no finger pressed anything.

- **Which window takes the keyboard.** A `[.titled]` sheet is the key window while it is up
  and its parent stays main; AppKit draws it with no title bar. A borderless window is
  never key as a sheet (its keys went to the parent's field) unless it says it can be. A
  panel and a full-size-content window behave as the titled one.
- **Focus.** It arrives with its first text field holding the caret. Tab goes round its
  fields and nowhere else (Keyboard Navigation off: no button is a stop).
- **Escape and Command-period** press the button whose key equivalent is Escape. A sheet
  with no such button stays up: Escape only ends a field's editing.
- **Return**, with the window or a button first responder, presses the default button,
  which a key sheet draws in the accent (`#007aff` against `#efefef`); on a focused
  button that is not the default it presses the default, and Space presses the focused
  one. With the caret in a field the field ends its editing, sends its action if it has
  one, and AppKit then hands the same Return to the window's key equivalents. Under a
  synthesized key the default button did not take it there (it took it when handed it
  outside that moment), which is not explained; a SwiftUI sheet under the same keys
  pressed its confirmation action from a field, and dismissed on Escape without pressing
  its Cancel.
- **Under it.** A click on the parent does nothing; its close button is disabled; a key
  sent to it goes to the sheet. The parent keeps its own first responder and selection.
- **Its place.** Centred on the parent's frame on both axes, and kept centred when its
  own size or the parent's changes. No part of one stands above the bottom of the parent's
  title bar, and AppKit leaves no margin around a large one: a sheet as large as the
  content area covers it exactly, and a larger one overhangs the window. The parent under
  it is `#cccccc` where it was white.
- **Arriving and leaving.** `beginSheet` and `endSheet` each return when the sheet's
  animation has run, about 270 ms later, whatever its `animationBehavior` or the animation
  context says. The completion handler runs at the start of the leaving. Nothing made the
  parent key again afterwards in this process.
- **What nests.** An alert begun on the sheet's own window is a sheet on the sheet. A
  second sheet, or an alert, begun on the parent while its sheet is up waits unseen until
  the first has gone.
- **A popover** takes the keyboard too: its first field has the caret as it arrives, Tab
  stays in it, and when it closes the parent has its first responder back. `.transient`
  closes on Escape, on a click anywhere else (at mouse-down on empty ground, and the click
  still reaches what it was on), when another window of the app becomes key and when the
  app is no longer active; a click on the button it is anchored to closes it and that
  button's action is not sent. `.semitransient` closes on Escape and not for another
  window or the app (a click through the queue was not tried on it); `.applicationDefined`
  for none of them. A click sent
  without the queue closed none, which is how the driver's clicks arrive. Shown while the
  app is not active, a transient one stays. Its arrow is inside the 13-point margin (10
  deep, 3 from the window's edge), its body's corner about 20; asked for an edge with no
  room on the screen it takes another, and it may leave its window. Against an anchor it
  cannot see (a hidden view, one scrolled out of sight, a window not on screen) AppKit
  shows no popover at all. Shown, it moves with its window and not with its anchor: the
  anchor scrolled 60 points and the popover stayed where it was.

**A list and its detail, at every width** (stage 6a, 2026-10-12, by the same method; its
probes, numbers and 95 pictures are in `…/mac-structure/stage-6a/`). **A Mac window of this
shape is always split.** Asked for every width from 1400 points down to 100, a SwiftUI
`NavigationSplitView` and an AppKit `NSSplitViewController` kept their columns side by
side: no item collapsed, SwiftUI's `columnVisibility` stayed `.all` and its horizontal size
class `regular`, and no Back appeared. The window stops instead:

| | The window stops at | Because |
|---|---|---|
| SwiftUI, sidebar, list and detail | 401 | sidebar 140, list 200, a 1-point divider, and 60 for the detail's own content |
| SwiftUI, two columns | 204 | its first column is a sidebar whatever it holds (144), and 60 |
| AppKit, content list and detail | 201 | the list's 200 and the divider: AppKit gives a detail no minimum, and it reaches zero |
| AppKit, with a sidebar | 345 | 144, 200 and the divider |
| AppKit, the detail given a minimum of 300 | 501 | 200, the divider and 300 |

What gives first is the detail, then the list down to its 200, then the sidebar down to its
144. Toolbar items go into the overflow menu before any column goes.

**Two headers in one window** (the same stage). With a title, buttons and a search field
declared on the list's column and on the detail's:

- **Three columns, SwiftUI.** The window's title is the list's ("Library", over the list's
  column). The list's button sits at the trailing end of the list's column, a tracking
  separator follows the divider between list and detail, and the detail's buttons begin
  the detail's column. The detail's title is not shown.
- **AppKit** with a second `NSTrackingSeparatorToolbarItem` on that divider lays out the
  same, and the items follow the divider as it moves; narrowing, the search field becomes a
  button first (at 640), the detail's items go to the overflow menu next (540), and the
  list's last (460).
- **Two columns, SwiftUI,** does the other thing: the detail's title and buttons have the
  bar, and the list's button is in the overflow menu.
- **One search field**, at the bar's trailing end, 325 wide, whichever column declared it.
  Declaring `.searchable` on both columns ends the process: `NSToolbar` refuses a second
  search item.
- **Keys.** With ⌘N and Return declared in both columns and the event handed to the
  window, the list's fired in three columns and the detail's in two, whichever view held
  the focus.

Not measured this way: a person's drag of a divider or a window's edge (a sidebar item says
it can collapse from a window resize; a list and a detail say they cannot), a real SwiftUI
scene rather than a hosting controller, key dispatch in a key window, and Mail, Notes and
Finder themselves.

**What Exact does today.** A `dialog` is a card Exact paints on an 18% backdrop inside the
window; a modal route is un-hidden with no surface at all; a popover is clipped to the
window; `NSPopover` and `beginSheet` are used nowhere but the alert. Covers, the way a host
tells the kernel "this box is the platform's now" on iOS, are not wired on macOS.

## 2. Decisions

### D1 — The reference, chosen per presentation; what is used and what is painted

The reference for each piece is what a person sees in a hand-built Mac app on the same
macOS. Where AppKit's default and SwiftUI's differ, the choice is made per piece, by what
Mac apps show, and each decision below says which it took and why: the toolbar's mode and
the grouped form are SwiftUI's (AppKit's defaults there are a labelled 66-point bar and a
column form); tabs are **not** SwiftUI's default (D3). The pieces are chosen to make one
Mac app of one macOS: the toolbar, the sidebar, the table and the sheet are all macOS 27's,
and none is taken from an older look because it is easier to build.

What is the operating system's is **used**: the toolbar, the split view and its sidebar, a
table, a sheet's window, a popover, the alert. **A control Exact uses keeps that control's
own metrics**: nothing here restyles one to match a probe's picture of another framework.
What has no native view under it in a hand-built app is **painted in the Mac's measured
shape**: the grouped form, as SwiftUI itself paints it. Painted numbers are rows of the
kernel's existing schema with a column per platform, as colour roles and text styles are.

This amends one sentence of LLP 1115, which says the oracle on the Mac is AppKit (§5 Q7).

### D2 — A route's `header` is the window's title bar and toolbar

A header-shaped route is projected by the shared header rule (which a lane is moving into
shared code now, so that Android reads it too), with the Mac's branch of it: **there is no
large title on the Mac**, so a level-1 heading is a title like any other.

- **The window's title** is the authored document title when there is one, else the
  route's heading, else the app's name: the order LLP 1115.000 D4.1 gave the web's page
  title (§5 Q2). So Shelf's window says "Library", then the book's title.
- Its buttons are real `NSToolbarItem`s that press the authored buttons (so the glass and
  the search field are AppKit's); a `filled` one is `.prominent`; a button that opens a
  menu is a menu item; its search field is the toolbar's search item; a `tablist` of text
  tabs in a header is a centred segmented group. Back stays first, where wave 1 put it.
- **Precedence.** A toolbar the author declared for the window (`toolbarPlacement="window"`)
  wins over projection. A header holding something the rule cannot project **stays painted,
  whole**, as today's toolbar refuses a shape it does not know; a header is never half
  projected. The header's box leaves layout (D8's cover) only in the same step in which
  projection succeeded.
- The bar is `.unified` and `.iconOnly`, as SwiftUI's is: about 52 points on macOS 27, which
  is what AppKit gives that style and not a number Exact sets. **This corrects LLP 1115.000
  D7.9**, which said the toolbar takes the system's display mode: `.default` shows labels
  and makes the bar 66 points. (A person cannot turn labels on for a toolbar that does not
  offer Customize Toolbar, and this one does not, §4.)

Stage 1 waited for the header rule's move, which it made: the rule was iOS-only Swift.

**What the build settled** (stage 1; the measurements are §1's):

- *Which route.* The route the root shows. Under a modal route the window keeps the title
  and the items of the route it stands over, disabled, as a window does under a sheet; the
  modal route's own header is its sheet's (D7.4, since stage 6c).
- *The order of items* is SwiftUI's own, read from its toolbar: after Back the header's
  buttons before its heading, as navigational items ahead of the title; the tablist as the
  centred group, then a flexible space; the buttons after the heading, which the title's
  own stretch puts at the bar's end; then a flexible space and the search item, last.
- *What "cannot project" means.* Everything the header shows has a place in the bar, or
  none of it goes: the heading, buttons whose face is one symbol and a title, a tablist of
  two or more tabs that each show a title alone, and a native search input. A picture, a
  second line of text, a field, a pressable around the heading, a button drawn as a box (an
  avatar), a tab with a symbol, a search input the author draws (`appearance: none`) and a
  button whose popover is neither a menu nor an alert each keep the whole header painted,
  and the session's log says which. iOS projects more of these (a subtitle, an avatar, a
  pressable title); on the Mac each is a follow-up (`QUEUE.md`), not a half projection.
- *A menu* is the button's popover when it has a button menu's shape: an
  `NSMenuToolbarItem`, filled from the popover's rows as it opens, after the button's own
  `press`. A button that asks first (the alert's shape) is a plain item, since its alert is
  a sheet on the window.
- *The search field is the input's own.* `NSSearchToolbarItem.searchField` takes a field
  the app made, as SwiftUI gives it one of its own class. The node makes a field of its
  class for the bar and is its delegate there, so the value, the caret, every event, the
  session's field editor and the test id are the authored input's; nothing of the page's
  look is written to it. No second field copies the first.
- *The title* is the authored document title, else the heading, else the app's name, and
  a heading with no words leaves the app's name. A header kept painted projects no title
  either: the window keeps the app's name, since a title taken alone is half a projection.
- *A toolbar item is also a menu command*, as a declared toolbar's already was: File holds
  "Add Book". A menu item and the search item are not.
- *Each window's toolbar has an identifier of its own.* AppKit keeps every toolbar of one
  identifier in a process the same, item for item; all had one, which went unseen while
  every window's bar held only Back.
- *Under the driver* the authored header paints, and `--chrome platform` shows the bar.

### D3 — The root tab bar is a sidebar

**When the tab bar's placement is unsaid**, as LLP 1116.004 D1.1 has it for the web (an
authored placement is the author's and stays), the window's content becomes a split view
with a real sidebar built from the shared rule that finds the root's `tablist` and what
each tab shows. It is AppKit's source list with the system's row size style, so its rows
follow the person's sidebar size setting; it runs under the title bar; it has the toolbar's
sidebar button, View ▸ Show Sidebar and a tracking separator; its width is remembered; the
selection is the system's. The page beside it narrows: the tablist's box leaves layout and
nothing else in layout changes.
Explicit `appearance="none"` on that root tablist keeps the authored bar instead,
with its stacks intact; removing it restores the sidebar (Charlie, 2026-10-12,
#393; implementer: Codex appearance lane).

**On a narrow window**, as stage 2 measured it on macOS 27 (§1). r3 recalled that a split
view collapses or overlays a sidebar below a minimum width. In a window that is not full
screen it does neither. The rule Exact is built to:

- The page keeps the minimum width the window already had (the manifest's, 320 points
  unsaid), now as its pane's minimum. That is the one number Exact gives the split view.
- As the window narrows the page gives up width first, down to that minimum; then the
  sidebar narrows to AppKit's 144; then the window stops. So the window's minimum width is
  the two together (464 with nothing declared) while the sidebar shows, and the page's
  alone while it is hidden.
- The sidebar opens at AppKit's 144 and afterwards at the width the person left it.
- The toolbar's button and View ▸ Show Sidebar hide and show it in place at any width;
  shown again in a window narrower than both panes need, the window widens.
- Whether it was hidden is remembered with its width.

Full screen, where AppKit documents an automatic collapse and an overlay, is AppKit's and
was not measured.

**What the build settled** (stage 2):

- *Which tablist* is the kernel's rule, `Kernel::root_tabs` (LLP 1115.001 D11), which the
  host asks through `exact_root_tabs`. The Mac still hides and inerts panels by its views'
  reading (`NavigationTabs`, shared with iOS); where the two readings differ the tablist
  stays as authored and the session's log says both. They agree for Shelf.
- *Placement unsaid* is the web's rule (`unsaidBar`), answered with the kernel's tabs:
  the layout rows it reads do not cross to the views. It is asked when the root's tabs
  change, when the root's or the tablist's props or style are written, and when the root or
  a child of it moves, which a resize does. A root that turns its direction with nothing
  moving, while the sidebar already stands for its tablist, is not followed; the web's own
  rule is as sticky.
- *The author hides the tab bar* on a screen with `display: none` on the tablist, as the
  tabs recipe says. The sidebar is hidden with it, as iOS hides its tab bar, its button
  and separator leave the toolbar, and Show Sidebar is disabled; that hide is not saved as
  the person's.
- *A row* is the tab's symbol without a `.fill` ending, its label, and its badge as
  AppKit's own badge button. Nothing sets a row's height, inset, font or colour.
- *A row chosen presses its authored tab*, on the way down, and the table selects
  nothing itself: the selected row is the tab whose stack holds the route the root shows.
- *The toolbar keeps `.unifiedCompact`* until D2's stage: a sidebar sits under it as it
  does under `.unified` (§1).
- *The window is full-size-content before the split view is its content*
  (`ExactSidebarController.install(in:)`), which is the order a hand-built app has from
  its first frame: the first row then starts under the bar and not 10 points below it (§1).
- *View ▸ Show Sidebar is AppKit's to name.* The item is made once with that title and
  the split view controller's `toggleSidebar:`; AppKit renames it Hide Sidebar or Show
  Sidebar when it validates it, so a menu nobody has opened still reads Show Sidebar.
- *Under a modal route* the sidebar is under AppKit's sheet with the rest of the window
  and takes no click (since stage 6c). Under a route painted in the window (the driver's
  own chrome) it stays live, as the painted tab bar did.

**A sidebar's symbols are the accent**, as AppKit draws them. That is an exception to LLP
1115.000 D3.9 (an unsaid symbol on the Mac is the secondary label colour), for a sidebar
only, and D3.9 gains the sentence.

**A sidebar, not toolbar segments** (§5 Q1). SwiftUI's unstyled `TabView` on macOS 27 is a
segmented control in the toolbar, so either would pass for hand-built. The sidebar keeps the
symbols, leaves the toolbar's centre to a header's own tabs (D2), is what LLP 1116.004 D1
already made a wide web window, and is where a Mac app with several places puts them. Its
cost is plain in Shelf: for three places a sidebar is heavier than three segments.

### D4 — A list and its detail are a split view

The Mac half of LLP 1116.004 D1.2. **The rule is that document's, moved into shared code:**
a stack splits when a row of its root route pushes the route shown beside it, and
`navigationSplit` on the root still opts out, names the list's width, or opts in a list the
rule does not find. The web's implementation recognises such a root by a disclosure symbol
in a row; that symbol is paint (D5 hides it on the Mac), so the shared rule reads the push
and not the picture. The web moves to the shared rule afterwards, in a change of its own, once the Mac's
split has proved it.

On the Mac the two routes sit in a **native split view**: its panes hold the routes, its
divider is AppKit's (resizable, reachable by keyboard), and each pane reports its size to
layout (D8). The row whose press pushed the visible detail stays selected (D5); the detail
has no Back; a push from the detail goes deeper inside the detail's pane, with Back at the
bar's leading end (§5 Q10, ruled).

**When it splits** (§5 Q6): **always, with a minimum window width.** r3 proposed the web's
900 points, measured after the sidebar, until a probe said whether a Mac app of this shape
is always split; the owner accepted it on that condition, and stage 6a's probe is that
probe (§1). It supports "always": at no width did a SwiftUI or an AppKit split of a list
and a detail fall back to a stack with Back, in two columns or three, and nothing on the
Mac corresponds to a threshold. The window stops narrowing instead. So on the Mac the
shared rule decides *whether* a stack splits and no width decides *when*; the web and the
iPad keep their width (LLP 1116.004 D1.2). The window's minimum content width is the
sidebar's 144 when there is one, the list's 200, the divider, and a minimum for the
detail. That last number is Exact's to choose in stage 6b: AppKit gives a detail none (it
went to zero in a window of 201 points) and SwiftUI's is whatever the detail's content
needs (60 in the probe).

**Decided before stage 6b is built, not in it:** with two headers in view, whose title the
window shows, where each header's buttons go, and which pane the keyboard's commands belong
to. A split cannot ship without those. What a hand-built window shows was measured in stage
6a (§1), and the answer is brought to Charlie from it (§5 Q10).

### D5 — A vertical `list` is a table

LLP 1115.000 D16's Mac half, and the owner's "list rows becoming real cells". **The end
state is a real, view-based `NSTableView`** whose rows hold the runner's rows: selection on
mouse-down, drawn emphasized or not by the window's key state, type-select, the keyboard's
own row navigation, row dragging, and what VoiceOver knows of a table come from AppKit and
are not rebuilt. A row is as tall as its content, as SwiftUI's is; separators and the
selection are the table's own (so a hairline is a device pixel without Exact drawing it);
anything Exact paints inside a selected row takes the `SelectedItem` roles (LLP 1115.000
D3.7). **No disclosure chevron** on a row (§5 Q4). `appearance="none"` on the list opts out,
and a horizontal list is not a table, as D16 says. Shelf's library, a `scroll` of hand-built
rows with hand-drawn separators today, becomes a `list`.

**First, a gate.** The runner makes and removes rows as they scroll, and a table
wants to own that. Stage 3a shows whether a table can be fed by the runner: rows arriving
and leaving, heights from layout, ten thousand rows scrolling inside the frame budget,
selection surviving a row's reuse, a control inside a row still taking its own clicks. The
same stage measures what the probes could not: when a press on a row acts (down or up),
what the keys do in a key window, and what selection does when the person returns from a
push. Stage 3b does not start until 3a has passed. If it fails, this decision comes back with what failed; it does not quietly
become paint.

**As built and as measured (stage 3a, 2026-10-12): the gate passed with conditions, and
the frame budget is the first of them.** A table can be fed by the runner. `TableHost`
(`Mac/TableHostMac.swift`) puts a virtualized list's rows in a view-based `NSTableView`
that no app can reach yet; `TableFeedMacTests`, `TableFeedKeyMacTests` and
`TableFeedPerfMacTests` hold it over ten thousand rows of mixed heights, in a window
really made key. The table is a view inside the list's document, not the scroller's
document: AppKit asks a delegate for about 300 row heights and estimates the rest (301 of
10,000), so a table's own length is a guess, and the list's extent and offset stay the
runner's. Rows arrive and leave, heights are layout's, the selection stays with its item
across a row's reuse and the keyboard, and a button, a checkbox and a field in a row keep
their clicks, in a key window and for the click that brings a window forward.

What the measurements change in the text above:

- **A row is selected, and its press runs, when the mouse comes up**, not on mouse-down. A
  stock `NSTableView` and SwiftUI's lists on macOS 27.0.1, in a key window, do nothing
  while the button is down. A table subclass that overrides `mouseDown` selects as it goes
  down, which is what the earlier probe saw; the table here overrides no mouse method.
  `press` is the table's action.
- **The keys.** Arrows, Option-arrows, Home, End and the Page keys do what a stock table's
  do. Return, Enter, Space and Escape do nothing in either reference, and nothing here. An
  arrow key pushes in SwiftUI's list of navigation links, so a row whose press shows its
  detail is pressed by the arrow that reaches it; any other row is not. After a push and
  Back the row is still selected, as SwiftUI's stack leaves it.
- **Type-select finds mounted rows only.** A row's text is known while it is mounted; a
  stock table finds any row. The selection is likewise its item's only while its row is
  mounted. Stage 3b needs the runner to answer both.
- **The table's separator is a point, not a device pixel**: one pixel at 1x and two in a
  bitmap scaled by two (no 2x display was attached), inset to the row's text, absent
  beside a selected row. The selection is its row's height, 10 points in, rounded.
- **Ten thousand rows are not inside the frame budget at the tail.** On a Mac shared with
  other builds (load 13 to 16 on 18 cores at its quietest), with the runner and kernel in
  the development profile, the main thread's processor time for a frame of a fling at
  6,000 and 12,000 points a second was p95 10.4 and 13.2 ms and p99 15.3 and 17.5 ms
  against a 13.33 ms frame; the list as it is laid out today, p95 6.4 and 9.5, p99 8.9 and
  12.8. At a sustained 5,000 points a second neither is inside (over on 17% to 25% of
  frames, and 8% to 16%). The table costs a third to a half more of the main thread at the
  median. Stage 3b brings that down (a row's height told to AppKit only when it changes;
  a new row put straight into its cell) and measures again on a quiet Mac with a release
  kernel before an app's `list` is a table.

The probes, what they printed, the timings and the pictures are in
`~/audits/exact2-no-tells-2026-10-11/mac-table-gate/`.

LLP 1115.000 D16's sentence "a hairline separator inset to the text" describes iOS's row.
D16 gains: each host's row is its platform's, and the Mac's is this.

**The gate, ruled (Charlie, 2026-10-12, "rec ok").** Stage 3a's gate passed on six of its
seven items and missed the frame budget at the tail (its as-built text below has the
numbers). Stage 3b goes ahead with a condition: its first work is cutting the cost of
feeding the table, measured again on a quiet Mac with a release kernel, and if the table
then cannot hold the 95th percentile inside the 13.33 ms budget at a 12,000 pt/s fling,
the decision comes back before any app's list becomes a table.

### D7 — A `dialog` and a modal route are sheets; a popover is a popover

1. **Now, as a fix:** a modal route gets an opaque surface. Today the page beneath shows
   through it.
2. **A `dialog` that is not alert-shaped is a sheet**: `beginSheet` with a window holding
   the dialog's own subtree at the size layout gives it. Escape, `closedby`, the focus's
   return and the default button are as they are (exactly one `filled`, non-destructive
   button is the default). The alert shape stays `NSAlert`. The probe's sheet was a titled
   window; whichever kind is used must be shown to take the keyboard.
3. **A popover that is not a menu is an `NSPopover`** at the subtree's size, transient for
   `popover="auto"`, on the edge its `position-area` names.
4. **A modal route is a sheet.** With no authored size it **fits its content on both axes**
   under a maximum (the parent window's content area less the margin AppKit leaves around
   its own largest sheet, measured in stage 6a), as a SwiftUI sheet and
   Exact's own `dialog` do; an authored size wins. Its header is projected into the sheet as
   SwiftUI lays one out: the heading in a title row at the top, and **the two actions it can
   identify, the one that cancels and the `filled` one that confirms, in a row at the bottom
   right**, cancel first, confirm the default (§5 Q3). Anything else in the header stays
   where the author put it. While a sheet is up the menus' commands go to it.

Before stage 6c, an XCTest in a key window shows that the sheet takes the keyboard and that
Escape and the default button work there. Items 2 to 4 put Exact's own views in a second window, which the Mac host has not done
before: the driver's coordinates, focus and `tap` learn it, as Android's did for its windows.

**What the build settled (stage 5, items 2 and 3).**

- *The sheet's window* is `[.titled]`, the kind measured to take the keyboard (§1). Its
  content is the dialog's own subtree in the containing block of D8.2, **a maximum on each
  axis: the content area of the window the page is in**, since AppKit leaves no margin
  around a large sheet. An authored width is used as written and `100%` is all of the
  maximum; the dialog's insets are not read, because AppKit places the sheet. The window is
  given the used size that comes back and nothing is measured by hand. Where the dialog
  paints no ground the sheet's own shows.
- *The keyboard* is the dialog's as it was (LLP 1115.000 D7.3), reached through the
  session's own key route for an event in any of its windows: the focus on arrival is the
  `autofocus`, else the first stop, which is the field AppKit would pick; Tab goes round
  the dialog's stops; Return presses the one `filled`, non-destructive button after a
  field's edit is committed, and a field with a `submit` handler keeps it; Escape closes.
  Command-period closes too, as it presses an AppKit sheet's Cancel.
- *`closedby`.* Unwritten or `closerequest`: Escape and Command-period close it and a click
  on the window behind does nothing. `none`: neither key does; only the dialog's own
  buttons and the app close it. `any`: as `closerequest`, and a click on the window behind
  the sheet closes it, which no AppKit sheet does of itself and is what the author asked
  for. Two places this is not AppKit's own sheet: Escape closes a dialog that has no
  cancel button (HTML's close request; AppKit's stays up), and no `cancel` event is fired
  at the dialog on the Mac, in either presentation, as before (read in the code, not run).
- *The page under it* is inert as under a painted dialog, so it gives its focus up when
  the sheet opens, and has it back with a field's selection when it closes; AppKit's own
  parent would have kept it throughout.
- *What nests.* An alert asked for while a dialog is open is `NSAlert` on the dialog's
  sheet (it was no alert at all there before: its rows were inert under the open dialog).
  A dialog opened over a dialog is a sheet on the first one's. Closing a dialog closes what
  stands on its sheet first. A popover opened from a button in it is AppKit's popover on
  the sheet's window; as in HTML it is written inside the dialog.
- *A popover that is no menu* is an `NSPopover` holding its subtree in a block that is a
  maximum on each axis (the screen less AppKit's margin), its content the size of the
  popover's border box. `popover="auto"` is `.transient` and `popover="manual"`
  `.applicationDefined`. Its edge is its `position-area`: `top …` above its invoker,
  `right …` and `left …` beside it, anything else below; AppKit moves it when that edge has
  no room. The margin that faces the invoker stands between the two. What the page's own
  rules close still closes it (Escape after the `key` handlers, the driver's click
  elsewhere, the invoker's toggle, its own hide button, a batch that takes it or its
  invoker away), and what AppKit closes itself closes the page's popover with it. It takes
  the keyboard as AppKit's does, where the painted one left the focus at its invoker; an
  `autofocus` in it still wins. It is a Tab loop of its own. A menu's shape is the `NSMenu`
  it was, and a popover AppKit shows none of (its invoker out of sight, §1) is painted.
- *The driver.* `--chrome agent` paints both as before, and a menu's shape is painted under
  either chrome (in the sheet, when its invoker is in one). Under `--chrome platform` they are
  the sheet and the popover, and `tree`, `layout` (in the viewport's space, where a person
  sees the node over the page), `tap` and `type` by test id, `state focus`, `state dialog`
  (`presentation: "sheet"`), `back`, `tree --ax` (the page behind a sheet exposes nothing,
  and the sheet's content and each popover's are roots) and `screenshot <png> window` (the
  window server's composite of the window and every window attached to it) reach them. A
  plain `screenshot` is the viewport's own drawing and names what it leaves out.

**What the build settled (stage 6c, item 4).**

- *It is the dialog's sheet.* A modal route the root shows over the route beneath it is
  an entry of the same host, its node the route (`DialogHost`, `RouteSheetMac.swift`): the
  same titled window, the same block, keys and clicks, the focus on arrival (its
  `autofocus`, else its first stop) and its return, an alert or a dialog over it, the
  driver. Navigation names the routes that are sheets after each batch, and a sheet arrives
  and leaves after the batch, never inside it, since AppKit turns the main queue while it
  does. A modal route over a modal route is a sheet on its sheet.
- *Its size.* The block is a maximum on each axis: the window's content area, less the
  sheet's own rows. With no authored size the route fits its content (Shelf's form is 331
  by 398, and its sheet 331 by 512). An authored `width` or `height` is used as written,
  by the kernel's rule under a maximum (D8.2 item 4), so it wins over the content; it
  stops at the window's content area, where AppKit's own sheet of a fixed size overhangs
  its parent. **That is less than D8.2's "(is, at most)"**: a host is sent no layout row
  (`width`, `height`, `inset`), so it cannot give a block that *is* the authored size
  (`QUEUE.md`).
- *Its header*, read by the shared rule and laid out on the pixels of SwiftUI's sheet of a
  `navigationTitle`, a `.cancellationAction` and a `.confirmationAction` (470 by 226,
  `…/mac-structure/png/sheet-swiftui-new-book.png`; this stage's pictures and numbers are
  in `…/mac-route-sheet/`): the heading is a 13-point bold title, its ink 49 points in on
  a baseline 30 down, in a 48-point row with a line under it; the buttons' row is 64
  points under a separator, the buttons 24 high and one width (66 for Cancel and Save), 8
  apart, 20 from the trailing and the bottom edge, cancel first. The one that cancels is
  the stack's Back control (`navigationBack`) in the header. The one that confirms is the
  header's one prominent, non-destructive button (`filled`, `bordered-prominent` and the
  prominent glass styles, which AppKit draws as one accent button): it is the sheet's
  default button and answers Return. A button that says no word, or that opens a menu, is
  neither. Both are `NSButton`s that press the authored buttons.
- *What leaves layout.* The header is covered `Whole` when it shows nothing else. Else the
  heading and the two buttons are, each, and the rest of the header stays where the author
  put it, at the top of the sheet's content: a search field, a menu's button, a third
  action, a tablist.
- *Kept painted, whole*, in the window as before, the log saying why: a header whose
  heading is inside a pressable, since a sheet's title is not pressed. Also painted: under
  the driver's own chrome; with no window; and a route pushed over a modal one, with the
  modal one, since a sheet holds one route (`QUEUE.md`). A route with no header, or one
  that says `navigationBar="none"`, is a sheet of its content with no row of the sheet's.
- *What dismisses it* is its `closedby`, as a dialog's: unwritten or `closerequest`, Escape
  and Command-period; `none`, neither, and its Cancel has no Escape; `any`, a click on the
  window behind it too. The dismissal is the stack's way back (LLP 1115 D5: the Back
  control pressed, else the root's `navigate` with the location beneath, else the router's
  own `back`), so the app's action runs and the sheet leaves when the route is no longer
  the one shown; a disabled Back control refuses it and the log says so. The window's own
  Back (the toolbar's, ⌘[, the swipe) is disabled under the sheet, as its items are.
- *Its ground.* The compiler gives a modal route the page's ground (LLP 1116.009 D3), and
  the Mac's grouped form stands on the same. In a sheet that ground is the sheet's own,
  which AppKit draws: lighter than a window's in Dark Mode (about `#2d2b2a` over `#1e1e1e`
  on this Mac; it moved a level or two between pictures) and no colour a box can paint. Painted, it was a darker band between the
  sheet's rows. So a box in a sheet that was given the window's background, and whose
  author wrote none, paints none (`standsOnSheetGround`; a dialog's sheet too). A ground an
  author wrote is painted as written.
- *`ModalGround`* (stage 0) is the ground of a route painted in the window to which the
  compiler gave none, its presentation being one it could not read. Nothing else is left
  of it.
- *A route that leaves.* Destroyed with its sheet up, the sheet keeps a picture of what it
  showed while AppKit takes it away. One that is only deselected goes back under its
  authored parent, its header as authored, and navigation hides it when the sheet has gone.
- *Its place in its stack* is still its authored one for navigation (`BlockHost.authored`).
  A subtree in a container of its own has left its parent's views, and the root's key
  matched no route.
- *Tab* goes round the route's stops; with Keyboard Navigation on the sheet's buttons are
  stops after them, as an AppKit sheet's are.
- *The driver.* `--chrome agent` paints the route as before. Under `--chrome platform` a
  `tap` naming Cancel or the confirming button is the sheet's button's click (`native:
  "NSButton"`), `back` is its dismissal, `state navigation.sheet` names its presentation
  (a sheet, or painted and why), its title, its buttons and its size, `tree --ax` has the
  sheet's content as a root (the title, the route, AppKit's two buttons), and everything
  else is as in a dialog's sheet.
- *Beside the reference.* On its pixels in light: every row and button above, and the
  parent under it (`#d2d2d2` where it was white). Not the reference's: the sheet's size,
  which is the content's (SwiftUI's form has a width it prefers, and its sheet was 470
  wide); the content's ground (the sheet's `#ffffff`; SwiftUI's form paints `#fcfcfc`); the
  line under the title in Dark Mode (one pixel; SwiftUI's shadow is two); and there the
  grouped card's fill, `#272727`, measured on a window (D6) and darker than a sheet's
  ground, where SwiftUI's is lighter (`#333030` on `#2b2727`).

### D8 — What the kernel learns

1. **Covers are wired on macOS.** iOS tells the kernel a box is gone (`Whole`), its edges
   are covered (`Edges`), or its scroller is inset (`Inset`). A projected header and the
   tablist are `Whole`, reported in the step in which the projection happened. Nothing else
   is needed for D2, D3, D5, or D7's dialog and popover.
2. **One new fact: a subtree's containing block, given from outside.** A route in a split
   pane and a modal route in a sheet are laid out in a box that is not the window. The
   kernel takes **constraints** for a subtree and lays CSS out inside them; it learns
   nothing about sidebars, splits or sheets. Where the host puts the result, and how it
   clips it, is the host's and is separate.
   - A pane **reports its size** (and a new one when its divider moves).
   - A sheet gives **a maximum**, and layout returns the content's size, which the host
     gives the window. That is the dialog's shrink-to-fit today, with a ceiling.
   - Written down before it is built: nested boxes; a box that goes away; percentages and
     absolutely or fixed positioned children (they resolve against the box); viewport units
     (they stay the window's, as the iOS host's existing rule has it).
3. **A proof before the split is built** (stage 6a), on the Mac alone: one session, two
   resizable native panes and one sheet, with a percentage child, an absolutely positioned
   child, a form whose height depends on its width, and one case that overflows; the same
   constraints given to a browser as the oracle; and a measurement that a subtree whose
   constraints did not change does no work. The iPad's half of LLP 1116.004 D1 takes the
   same seam afterwards.
4. **The platform's metrics are data** (D6), in the existing schema.

Inside a box the web stays the layout oracle. The web's own split moves to the shared rule
later, in its own change (D4).

#### D8.2, written down: a containing block from outside (stage 6a, before it was built)

**The fact.** A host gives one node a **containing block**: a width and a height, and for
each whether it **is** the size (a pane) or is **at most** the size (a sheet). The node is
the root of the subtree the host shows in a view of its own. One fact serves both uses: a
split pane is (is, is), a sheet with no authored size is (at most, at most), and a form
sheet of a fixed width is (is, at most), which is what iOS's `fit-content` detent already
measures by other means (LLP 1075.003 §9.11).

**In CSS's terms.** While a node has a block:

1. **It is out of its parent's layout**, as a box in CSS's top layer is out of flow: its
   parent and siblings are laid out as though it were `display: none`. For a route, which
   is `position: absolute` already, nothing around it moves.
2. **It is laid out as a root is** (LLP 1001 §3), in its block instead of the viewport: the
   root element of an iframe, in an initial containing block of the frame's size. It is
   block-level whatever it lays its children out as; its percentages, its insets when it is
   absolutely positioned and its automatic margins resolve against the block; a `static`
   one is `relative`, as every root is (LLP 1074 T1), so it holds the absolutely positioned
   boxes no positioned box inside it holds.
3. **Where the block is the size**, nothing else is derived: `width: auto` fills it less the
   margins, `height: auto` is the content's, `position: absolute; inset: 0` fills it, and
   `height: 100%` is its height.
4. **Where the block is a maximum**, the root is sized on that axis as a modal `dialog` is
   in its viewport (`width: fit-content; max-width: 100%`; the dialog's shrink-to-fit
   today, with the host's ceiling for a containing block): an `auto` size is `fit-content`;
   its border box is at most the maximum, under any `max-width` or `max-height` of its own;
   its insets and automatic margins on that axis are `auto` and zero, because they place a
   box against an edge that is here the answer (the `fit-content` detent drops a route's
   `top` and `bottom` for the same reason). An authored size is used as written, a
   percentage of the maximum, still under it.
5. **Percentages below the root** are CSS's: each against its own containing block, so a
   child's `width: 50%` under a shrink-to-fit root is CSS's cyclic percentage (automatic for
   the content size, then half the used width).
6. **Positioned descendants** never resolve outside the subtree: an absolutely positioned
   box is placed against its nearest positioned ancestor, which at the furthest is the
   root. **`position: fixed` is not a row** (LLP 1001 §1), so nothing inside reaches the
   window; were it added, a block would contain it as a transformed ancestor contains a
   fixed box in CSS, so a fixed box in a sheet would be fixed to the sheet.
7. **Viewport units, `env()`, `rem` and what the runner reads of the window's width are the
   window's**, not the block's: `50vw` in a 300-point pane is half the window. This is the
   rule iOS already has for a sheet (LLP 1075.003 §9.11: `ExactViewIOS.fit` tells the
   kernel the window, `Env::screen`, and `ViewportUnit::basis` reads it), so no length
   follows a pane's or a sheet's size except through the containing block. The display's
   scale, which a hairline (`-exact-hairline`) resolves against, is the window's in the
   same way (added on 2026-10-12, when LLP 1115.000 D10.3 made it a layout input).
8. **Overflow.** Content larger than the root overflows the root, and the root's `overflow`
   says what happens, as for any box. Under a maximum the root stops at the maximum, so a
   route that is a flex column with a `flex: 1; min-height: 0` list shrinks the list, which
   scrolls. Where the block is the size, a root taller than it (an `auto` height with much
   content) overflows the block, and clipping or scrolling that is the host's, with the
   view it put the subtree in.
9. **A block inside a block.** A node with a block inside a subtree with a block leaves
   that subtree's layout the same way, and neither reads the other.
10. **A block that appears, changes or goes away** takes effect at the next layout. When
    it goes away the node returns to its parent's layout with the rows its author wrote,
    which never changed. No node is made or destroyed by any of it, so what belongs to
    nodes stays: a field's text, a scroll offset, the focus. A node that is destroyed
    takes its block with it.
11. **A hidden root** (`display: none` on it or on an ancestor) lays nothing out, as in
    CSS, and its used size is zero.

**What comes back.** Frames inside the subtree are in the block's own space, from its top
left corner; the page's frames are in the page's, as before. The block's **used size** is
the given length where the block is the size, and where it is a maximum the root's border
box plus its margins, at most the maximum. The kernel publishes it with the layout that
changed it, beside the root's frame. Feedback is bounded by construction: a pane's used
size is what it reported, and a sheet's maximum comes from its parent window, never from
the sheet, so the size layout returns is never an input to the layout that returned it.

**What does not change.** The author's rows (the kernel derives the engine's style from
them, as it does for a cover). The tree and its order, so focus, accessibility and
inheritance follow it across a block: a subtree in a sheet inherits its colour and font
from the page it was written in, unlike an iframe's document. The page around it: a block
that changes lays out its own subtree and nothing else (D8.3 measures that). The covers:
`Edges` and `Inset` on a root with a block apply inside the block, which the iPad's
columns, each under its own bar, will use.

**Which CSS model, and where each stops fitting.**

- **An iframe's initial containing block** is the model for the root and its percentages.
  It stops at the document: an iframe has its own viewport units, media queries, inherited
  values, focus scope and scroller, and it never sizes to its content. Here there is one
  document, and the window keeps the units.
- **A containing block established by a transform** (or `contain: layout`) is the model
  for positioned descendants, absolute and fixed alike, with the units left alone. It
  stops at size: such a box is still sized by its own parent, in flow.
- **Container size** (`contain: size`) is the model for a pane's isolation: the size comes
  from outside and nothing inside changes anything outside, which is why an unchanged
  subtree does no work. It stops at the sheet, whose size is its content's.
- **A modal `dialog`** (the top layer, `fit-content` under a maximum) is the model for
  leaving the flow and for the sheet. It stops at placement: a dialog is centred by its own
  insets and margins in the viewport, and here the host places the view.

**Declared, not built in stage 6a.** A registered content region and a block are refused
together, as a content region and a presented height are. A text exclusion (`wrap-flow`)
does not shape text across a block's edge, in either direction. Only the Apple host reads
the fact; every other host lays out as before, since a host that gives no block changes
nothing.

**As built (stage 6a, 2026-10-12).** `Kernel::set_host_block` and `host_block_size`
(`kernel/src/kernel/block.rs`), reached through the covers' door (`exact_host_covers`,
record kinds 4 and 5), with a `block` op that carries the used size back beside the root's
frame. Building it changed one thing the text above does not say: under a maximum width a
root that is in flow is laid out as an absolutely positioned box at its static position.
CSS gives both the same size, and the engine sizes such a box before it lays its children
out, which is what gives their percentages a basis.

The block is the second fact a host gives layout that day. The display's scale (LLP
1115.000 D10.3) is the window's and is resolved into rows when a batch commits, as the root
font size is; a block is one node's, changes no row and takes effect at the next layout,
as a cover does. So the two do not meet: a hairline in a block is the display's device
pixel, and a new scale lays out a block's subtree through the rows it moved and names
nothing of the page around it
(`a_hairline_in_a_block_is_the_displays_and_a_new_scale_lays_that_subtree_out`).

- **The fixtures are Chrome's** (`kernel/tests/it/browser_host_block.rs`): 27 cases, each

  layout and has them taken back, and is bit for bit a kernel that had them from the start

- **An unchanged subtree does no work.** On a page of two panes and a sheet, 30 paragraphs
  each (97 nodes): a pane going from 300 to 240 points measures 90 paragraphs, all its own,
  and publishes its 31 nodes; the other pane, the sheet and the page measure, lay out and

- **The Mac's proof** is `HostBlockMacTests`: one session, the two panes of an

- **What the host had to do**, in `BlockHost` (`Mac/BlockHostMac.swift`): move the
