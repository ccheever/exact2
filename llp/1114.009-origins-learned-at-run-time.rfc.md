# LLP 1114.009: Reaching origins the app learns at run time

**Type:** RFC
**Status:** Draft, 2026-10-08. Not reviewed.
**Systems:** the grant grammar (`grants/src/lib.rs`, re-exported by `vendor/ibex/crates/ibex2/src/grant.rs`); the web's admission (`host/web/navigation.js`, shared by `host/web-js/admission.js` and `host/web/http-body.js`) and its parity corpus (`host/web/tests/fixtures/grants.json`); `auth.session` admission (`runner/src/auth.rs`); the render server's CSP (`host/render/src/serve.rs`); the reach table (`bake/src/reach.rs`, the deploy dry run, the install page); `docs/contract-for-agents.md`. Every executor of `net.fetch`: Apple (iOS, macOS, tvOS), Linux, Windows, both web targets.
**Author:** Claude (Opus 5.5) for Charlie Cheever
**Date:** 2026-10-08
**Implementer:** unassigned until ruled
**Related:** LLP 1114 (the parent: what the Bluesky clone asks of Exact2), gap-audit item 86; LLP 1016 D6 (grants name the hosts an app may reach) and its 2026-09-26 amendment; LLP 1054 D7 and LLP 1054.000 R5 (Charlie's ruling on subdomain grants); LLP 1069.006 D2 and §"Discovery" (`auth.session`, the AT Protocol sign-in); LLP 1069.008 D7 (the reach table) and §"What stays out"; LLP 1027.001 (a language change never grants a capability); `rules/DEFERRED.md:400` (durable capability grants)

## 1. Summary

A Bluesky account lives on a PDS whose origin the app learns at sign-in. A
grant names its origins before the app runs. Exact2 already admits every
Bluesky-hosted PDS through `net.fetch https://*.host.bsky.network`. It
can't admit a PDS run by anyone else.

This RFC weighs four answers and recommends the first: an **open grant**,
`net.fetch https://*`, spelt as CSP spells it. It admits any public HTTPS
origin on port 443 and nothing else. It's printed at the top of the reach
table, so a reviewer sees it. A prompt per origin, a grant bound to DID
resolution, and a proxy all cost more and protect less. The problem isn't
Bluesky's alone. Mastodon, Matrix, JMAP, CalDAV, RSS and podcast feeds all
learn their server from the user, so the answer is meant to be general.

## 2. What Bluesky does

The source says fetch grants are static origins, so self-hosted accounts
can't be reached. That's half right.

**How the client finds the server** (AT Protocol: [handle](https://atproto.com/specs/handle),
[DID](https://atproto.com/specs/did), [OAuth](https://atproto.com/specs/oauth)):

1. **Handle to DID.** The client resolves the handle by a DNS TXT record
   (`_atproto.<handle>`) or by `https://<handle>/.well-known/atproto-did`.
   A client without DNS can call `com.atproto.identity.resolveHandle` on a
   service it trusts instead.
2. **DID to document.** A `did:plc` resolves at `https://plc.directory/<did>`.
   A `did:web` resolves at `https://<domain>/.well-known/did.json`, which
   can be any domain.
3. **Document to PDS.** The `#atproto_pds` service's `serviceEndpoint` is
   the PDS origin.
4. **PDS to authorization server.** For OAuth, the client reads the PDS's
   `/.well-known/oauth-protected-resource`, then the authorization server's
   metadata. For Bluesky-hosted accounts that server is the `bsky.social`
   entryway. A self-hosted PDS is usually its own.
5. **Every signed-in call goes to the PDS.** The access token's audience is
   the PDS. The PDS forwards `app.bsky.*` calls to the AppView (service
   proxying, [XRPC](https://atproto.com/specs/xrpc)).

So the client reaches an origin it learned in step 3, and maybe in steps 1
and 2. Bluesky's own clients (React Native, and `bsky.app` in a browser)
reach any PDS without asking anyone.

**The correction.** Since 2026-09-26 a grant can name every host under one
domain (`scheme://*.domain`, LLP 1054.000 R5). The port already grants
`https://*.host.bsky.network` (LLP 1069.006, "Discovery"). That covers
every account Bluesky hosts. What fails is an account on a PDS outside
Bluesky's domains: a third-party host or a self-hosted one. App passwords
through the entryway don't reach those accounts either, since the entryway
forwards only for Bluesky's own PDSes (LLP 1054 D7).

## 3. What exact2 has today

**The grammar** (`grants/src/lib.rs`, one crate for every executor;
LLP 1027.001 keeps it one):

- A `net.fetch` grant is an origin matched whole (`:24-29`), or every host
  strictly under one domain of two labels or more, at the grant's scheme
  and port (`Grant::FetchSubdomains`, `:163-170`, `:180-190`).
- `*` is refused anywhere else. `net.fetch https://*` fails to parse with
  "`*` is allowed only as `net.fetch scheme://*.domain`" (`:375-379`).
  The comment on `subdomains` says why the floor is two labels: "`*.com`
  would be every site" (`:441-444`).
- A grant set is "fixed at creation, because a grant set that can be added
  to after the fact is ambient authority wearing a struct (LLP 0067 §2)"
  (`:320-322`).

**Enforcement, per executor:**

- **Native** (Apple, Linux, Windows): the host endows ibex2's bindings once,
  from the app's grant text (`host/apple/src/store.rs:102-108`,
  `host/linux/src/executor.rs:35`). `fetch_stream` admits every hop,
  redirects included (`vendor/ibex/crates/ibex2/src/stdlib/fetch.rs:380-420`).
  A cross-origin redirect drops `Authorization`, `Cookie` and
  `Proxy-Authorization` (`:476-483`).
- **Web, both targets**: `admitsNetwork` checks the same typed tuples the
  Rust parser sealed (`host/web/navigation.js:1358-1368`). The JS target
  calls it before `fetch` and again on the final URL after a redirect
  (`host/web-js/admission.js:73`, `:93-98`). The wasm target does the same
  in `host/web/http-body.js:154`, `:225`. The file says what this is: "App
  code is the page, so this is parity admission rather than a sandbox"
  (`navigation.js:1159-1160`).
- **Served web**: the render server writes `connect-src` from the
  `net.fetch` and `net.websocket` lines (`host/render/src/serve.rs:1447-1463`,
  `:1486`). It copies each target's origin verbatim, so
  `https://*.host.bsky.network` reaches the CSP as written. A static dist
  sends no CSP (LLP 1069.008 D6).
- **Sign-in**: `auth.session` uses `net.fetch`'s grammar for its target,
  re-implemented in `origin_admits` (`runner/src/auth.rs:229-245`), and
  `check_grants` refuses an authorization URL on any other origin
  (`:299-311`).

**Review.** The reach table lists every `net.fetch` line as "runtime (all
hosts); CSP (served web)" (`bake/src/reach.rs:297`). The deploy dry run and
the install page print it (LLP 1069.008 D7). Adding a grant line changes
the grant ceiling, so the classifier sends it to a binary release
(`scripts/app.mjs:1361-1363`).

**What's ruled out.** LLP 1069.008 "What stays out" (`:291-293`) refuses
asking for more at run time, and grants per user or per install.
`rules/DEFERRED.md:400` defers durable capability grants.

## 4. What the web does

A web page has no per-origin network permission. Its reach has two limits:

- **The server's consent.** [CORS](https://fetch.spec.whatwg.org/#http-cors-protocol)
  lets a page read a cross-origin response only if the server allows it.
  PDSes allow it, which is how `bsky.app` signs in to any PDS from a
  browser.
- **The author's restriction.** [CSP `connect-src`](https://www.w3.org/TR/CSP3/#directive-connect-src)
  limits where the page may connect. Its host-source grammar has
  `https://*` (any host, HTTPS, the default port) and the scheme-source
  `https:` (any host, any port). A policy can only be tightened at run time,
  never widened. A second policy, from a `<meta>` or a header, narrows the
  first.

The user is asked about network reach in two places only:

- **Chrome's [Local Network Access](https://wicg.github.io/local-network-access/)**
  prompts before a public page reaches a private address.
- **Browser extensions** declare `host_permissions`, and may ask for more at
  run time with `optional_host_permissions` and `permissions.request`.
  The Chrome Web Store reviews broad host permissions such as `<all_urls>`
  more closely.

So the web offers both of the shapes below: (a), a broad declared reach that
a reviewer sees, and (b), a declared ceiling plus a prompt. Plain pages use
neither and are bounded only by CSP. Native platforms don't restrict origins
at all. iOS's App Transport Security checks the TLS, not the host.

## 5. Design

### Options

**(a) An open grant, with a review flag.** `net.fetch https://*` admits any
HTTPS origin. The reach table shows it first and plainly.

- *For:* one new grant kind; no state, no prompt, no host UI; the CSP
  already has the spelling; the review promise stays honest ("this app can
  reach any site"); it generalizes to every federated protocol.
- *Against:* for `net.fetch`, the grant no longer bounds a data module that
  holds it. It goes past the two-label floor Charlie set on 2026-09-26.

**(b) A ceiling plus a prompt, persisted.** The grant says "origins the user
approves". The first fetch to a new origin asks "Allow Bluesky to connect to
pds.example.org?", and the host keeps the answer per install.

- *For:* each origin is the user's decision, which is what extensions do.
- *Against:* the app chooses which origin to ask about, and a person can't
  judge a PDS host name, so the prompt is consent theatre. A malicious app
  asks for `pds-eu.example` and gets it. Bluesky's own clients show no such
  prompt, so the clone gains a step the real app lacks.
- *Cost:* it reverses the LLP 0067 §2 principle the grammar cites
  (`grants/src/lib.rs:320-322`) and LLP 1069.008's "What stays out". It
  moves "durable capability grants" off `rules/DEFERRED.md`. It needs a
  per-install store on every host, a prompt on every host (the web
  included, where admission is parity and not a sandbox), and an agent
  substitute (LLP 1069.007 D2).

**(c) A grant bound to a resolution rule.** For example, "origins named as
`#atproto_pds` in a DID document fetched from `https://plc.directory`". The
runtime admits an origin only after it has seen it in such a document.

- *For:* the manifest still names a rule a reviewer can read.
- *Against:* the bound is weak. Anyone can publish a `did:plc` whose PDS
  endpoint is any URL, so the rule admits any origin someone published.
  `did:web` puts the document itself on an arbitrary domain, so the
  resolver isn't bounded either. The runtime has to parse DID documents and
  keep what it resolved: protocol knowledge and state in the executor,
  three times (Rust, JS target, wasm glue).
- *It doesn't generalize.* Mastodon, Matrix (`/.well-known/matrix/client`),
  JMAP (`/.well-known/jmap`) and RSS start from a domain the user types.
  There's no granted resolver to bind to.

**(d) A proxy.** The app reaches only its own server, which forwards to the
PDS.

- *For:* grants stay exact. An app can already do this today.
- *Against:* it moves the open reach to a server and doesn't bound it. Every
  user's DPoP-bound tokens and data pass through a third party. It adds the
  latency Charlie ruled against on 2026-09-26 ("going through the
  bsky.social entryway might be causing unnecessary latency"). It needs a
  service, which exact2 doesn't run for apps. On the web it solves nothing,
  since CORS already allows the direct call.

### D1 — The open grant: `net.fetch https://*` (recommended: option a)

A new grant kind, `Grant::FetchAny`, with exactly one spelling.

- **It admits** `https`, port 443, any host that is a DNS name of two
  labels or more.
- **It refuses** `http`, any other port, IP literals (v4 and v6) and
  single-label names such as `localhost`. Those stay explicit grants, as
  the auth fixture's `net.fetch http://127.0.0.1:4331` is
  (`apps/auth-fixture/app.ts:16`).
- **Fetch only.** `net.websocket` stays exact, as subdomain grants are
  (`grants/src/lib.rs:617`). A protocol that needs sockets to learned hosts
  (Nostr relays, Mastodon streaming) is the same decision for
  `net.websocket wss://*`, taken when an app asks.
- **The spelling is CSP's.** `https://*` is a CSP host-source with the same
  meaning: any host, HTTPS, the default port. The parser takes no other
  spelling: not `https://*:*`, not `*`, not `https:`.

*Why the floor:* the refusals follow the web's own direction (Local Network
Access) and keep the open grant to the public internet. A name that
resolves to a private address (DNS rebinding) is a residual risk on native
hosts. On the web, the browser handles it.

*Why not (b), (c) or (d):* see above. Each keeps the look of a bound
without the substance, and costs a store, a prompt, a parser or a server.
(a) says what is true and costs a grant kind.

### D2 — `auth.session https://*`, the same line for sign-in

A self-hosted PDS is usually its own authorization server, so the clone
also needs to open a sign-in session there. `origin_admits`
(`runner/src/auth.rs:229-245`) accepts `https://*` with D1's rules.

- The person sees the origin in the system's browser sheet
  (`ASWebAuthenticationSession`) or the web's popup, so this line carries
  less risk than the fetch line.
- `auth.callback` stays exact. The callback is the app's own and never
  learned.
- While here, `origin_admits` should call the grants crate instead of
  repeating its grammar. That way a third grammar can't drift from the
  other two.

### D3 — What still holds under the open grant

- **Every hop is admitted.** Redirects are checked natively at each hop
  (`fetch.rs:380-420`) and on the web at the final URL (`admission.js:93-98`).
  A redirect to `http://10.0.0.1` is still refused.
- **Credentials don't follow a redirect.** A cross-origin redirect drops
  them (`fetch.rs:476-483`; the web's `fetch` does the same for
  `Authorization`).
- **Other grant lines keep their meaning.** `secret.keep`, `fs.*`,
  `sqlite.open`, `net.websocket`, `device.*` and `auth.callback` are
  unchanged. The open line widens `net.fetch` and nothing else.
- **Scope it in a mixed app.** A source's scope may select only some of the
  app's lines (`runner/src/auth.rs:249-264`, `navigation.js` `scopedGrantSet`).
  In a mixed app, the open line can belong only to the source that talks to
  the PDS.

### D4 — The review flag

The reach table (`bake/src/reach.rs`) gives the open line its own row
text, and every surface that prints the table puts it first:

```
reach                      purpose                    enforced by
net.fetch https://*        any public https site      runtime (all hosts); CSP https://* (served web)
auth.session https://*     sign in at any https site  the host's exact-auth: arm (Apple, web; Linux answers 501)
net.fetch https://plc.directory  —                    runtime (all hosts); CSP (served web)
```

- **The deploy dry run** prints one line above the table: "This app may
  fetch from any public https origin (`net.fetch https://*`)."
- **The install page** says "Connects to any website", in plain words, as
  LLP 1069.008 D7 does for every row.
- **A binary release.** Adding the line moves the grant ceiling, so the
  classifier already sends it to a binary release. No new classifier rule
  is needed.
- **No extra gate.** I recommend no confirmation flag beyond that (§6 Q3).
- **Keep the exact lines.** The app's exact lines (`plc.directory`,
  `*.host.bsky.network`) stay legal beside the open one. They no longer
  widen anything, but they still tell a reader what the app normally talks
  to. The bake doesn't warn about them.

### D5 — Per host

- **Web, served.** `connect-src` gains `https://*`. `csp()` copies the
  target verbatim (`serve.rs:1453-1461`), so this needs a test and no new
  code. CORS remains the server's consent.
- **Web, static dist.** No CSP, as today. The JS admission is parity, as it
  is for every grant.
- **Web admission.** `networkTuple`, `grantTupleValid` and `admitsNetwork`
  (`navigation.js:1193-1238`, `:1358-1368`) gain a `fetch-any` tuple.
  `normalized_json` (`runner/src/grants.rs:170-216`) emits it. The parity
  corpus (`host/web/tests/fixtures/grants.json`, `grants/tests/parity.rs`)
  gets D1's admits and refusals, run by both parsers.
- **Apple** (iOS, macOS, tvOS). NSURLSession under App Transport Security.
  A PDS whose TLS fails ATS fails with the transport's error, not
  `Refused`. That's the platform's rule, and the error names it.
- **Linux, Windows.** The rustls transport with its trust roots
  (`host/linux/src/executor.rs:25-31`). The behaviour is the same.
- **Terminal.** Same as Linux wherever it runs a data module's fetch.
  Nothing specific.

### D6 — The agent's view

There is no prompt, so there is no substitute to add (LLP 1069.008,
"Substitute"). A drive verifies the admission rule directly:

- **An open fetch is admitted.** A fetch to `https://pds.example.invalid`
  answers a network failure (DNS), not `Refused`. `state` lists the request
  and `logs` has no grant refusal.
- **The floor refuses.** A fetch to `http://pds.example.org` or
  `https://10.0.0.1` answers `Refused`, and `logs` names the grant it lacks.
- **Faults work as before.** `--fail-fetch https://pds.` (LLP 1103) fails
  the PDS hop on purpose, to drive the clone's "can't reach your server"
  state.

A real self-hosted PDS is a manual check (§7), since a drive shouldn't sign
in to a live account.

### General case

Each federated protocol learns its server from the user or from a document
at a domain the user named:

- Mastodon and ActivityPub: an instance domain, then WebFinger.
- Matrix: `/.well-known/matrix/client` on the user ID's server.
- JMAP and CalDAV: `/.well-known/jmap`, `/.well-known/caldav`.
- Email autoconfig, RSS and podcast feeds, and self-hosted anything
  (Nextcloud, Home Assistant).

(c) needs a new rule for each of these, and there's often no resolver to
bind to. (b) puts a prompt in front of each one. (a) is one line for all of
them. An app that only talks to known servers keeps exact grants and loses
nothing.

## 6. Questions for Charlie

1. **Admit an open grant at all?** It goes past your 2026-09-26 ruling's
   floor ("nothing wider" than one domain; `grants/src/lib.rs:27-28`,
   `:441-444`; LLP 1016 D6's amendment). *Recommend yes,* for the reasons
   in §5. You ruled on subdomains that "the developer should just use it
   carefully". The review flag is what makes that visible.
2. **Spelling and floor:** `https://*` (443 only, no IP literals, no single
   label), or CSP's `https:` (any port)? *Recommend `https://*`.* A
   self-hosted PDS on another port is rare, and admitting any port admits
   every service on every host.
3. **A gate on first use?** Should a production deploy that first adds
   `https://*` need an explicit acknowledgement, such as an `app.json` key
   or a deploy flag? *Recommend no.* It's already a binary release with a
   loud row in the table. A second declaration is what LLP 1069.008 set
   out to remove.

## 7. What the clone does after

1. **Grants.** Add `net.fetch https://*` and `auth.session https://*`. Keep
   `https://plc.directory`, `https://*.host.bsky.network` and
   `https://bsky.social` as documentation.
2. **Handle resolution.** Exact2 has no DNS API on any host, so resolve
   through `com.atproto.identity.resolveHandle` on a granted service, or
   `https://<handle>/.well-known/atproto-did`, which the open grant now
   admits.
3. **DID resolution.** `did:plc` at `plc.directory`. `did:web` at
   `https://<domain>/.well-known/did.json`. Read the `#atproto_pds`
   endpoint, and check that the handle the document names resolves back to
   the same DID.
4. **OAuth discovery against the PDS.** Use LLP 1069.006's sequence with
   the learned origin in place of `*.host.bsky.network`.
5. **Keep the PDS origin** in the session record under `secret.keep`, and
   send every signed-in call there.

**Verified by:**

- The grants crate's and the web's parity tests for D1.
- A drive against a fixture "self-hosted" DID document that names a PDS
  outside Bluesky's domains (§5 D6). The request is admitted, and its
  failure is the network's, not `Refused`.
- By hand, with Charlie's OK: sign in to one real non-Bluesky PDS on iOS
  and on the web, then read the timeline and post.

## 8. Effort and complexity

**Exact2: S.** About a day.

- **The grant kind:** one variant and its parse and admit in
  `grants/src/lib.rs`, its tuple in `navigation.js` and `runner/src/grants.rs`,
  and corpus rows.
- **Sign-in:** `origin_admits` delegated to the grants crate.
- **Review:** the reach row and the dry-run line, plus a CSP test.
- **Docs:** a line in `docs/contract-for-agents.md` beside `net.fetch`
  (`:683-686`, which doesn't yet mention subdomain grants either).
- **The hard part** is keeping three parsers (Rust, the web's validator,
  `auth.rs`) agreeing on the floor (IP literals, single labels), and the
  parity corpus is what proves it.
- **Easy:** the CSP needs no new code, there's no host UI, and there's no
  state.

**Clone: S–M.**

- **Resolution and discovery** generalized from Bluesky's domains to any
  origin, with handle and DID verification. This is most of the work, and
  it's protocol work the clone needs anyway.
- **Moving off app passwords** to OAuth (LLP 1069.006) is a prerequisite
  for self-hosted accounts on the web, not part of this RFC.

**Rulings.** This reverses nothing on `rules/DEFERRED.md`. It extends LLP
1054.000 R5 and LLP 1016 D6 past their floor (§6 Q1). Choosing (b) instead
would reverse LLP 1069.008 "What stays out" and move "durable capability
grants" off `rules/DEFERRED.md:400`.

## 9. Not in scope

- **Wildcard sockets** (`net.websocket wss://*`), until an app asks.
- **Grants learned at run time, prompts, and per-install grant state**
  (option b). They stay deferred.
- **A DNS API** for handle resolution by TXT record. The web has none, so
  exact2 has none.
- **Image and video sources from learned origins.** `img-src` and
  `media-src` already allow `https:` (`serve.rs:1486`). These aren't
  `net.fetch`.
- **Hardening against DNS rebinding** on native hosts, past D1's refusal of
  IP literals.
